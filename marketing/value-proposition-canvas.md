# Value Proposition Canvas

A two-sided tool that forces an explicit match between what a business offers (the Value Proposition) and what a specific customer segment actually needs (the Customer Profile) — used inside STP's Differentiation step to make sure a claimed USP is actually anchored in a real customer pain or gain, not just a feature the business is proud of.

## When to use

Right after a target segment has been chosen in STP (see [stp-model.md](stp-model.md)) and before finalizing the positioning statement or the Product decisions in the 4Ps (see [marketing-mix-4ps.md](marketing-mix-4ps.md)). Use it any time a client's pitch describes the product in isolation ("we make X") without describing what customer problem X solves.

## How it works

Two mirrored halves, read right-to-left (understand the customer first, then design the offer to match):

**Customer Profile** (the target segment, described in their own terms):
- **Gains** — the outcomes and benefits the customer wants.
- **Pains** — the frustrations, risks, and obstacles the customer experiences before/during/after trying to get their job done.
- **Jobs-to-be-done** — the tasks the customer is trying to accomplish, functionally or emotionally, that create the need for gains and pains in the first place.

**Value Proposition** (the product, described in terms of what it does *for* that profile):
- **Products & Services** — the concrete list of what's actually offered.
- **Gain Creators** — how the product/service produces the gains the customer wants.
- **Pain Relievers** — how the product/service eliminates or eases the specific pains identified.

The canvas only "fits" when every Gain Creator maps to a real Gain and every Pain Reliever maps to a real Pain — a Gain Creator with no corresponding Gain, or a product feature with no Pain Reliever attached to it, is a sign of a feature built for its own sake rather than for the customer.

## Example — Handicraft Company in Egypt (worked case from the source material)

**Customer Segments**: Primary — American tourists aged 30-50 visiting Luxor for the first time; Secondary — European tourists visiting major destinations; Potential — online buyers seeking authentic Egyptian handicrafts.

**Customer Pains**: difficulty finding authentic souvenirs; lack of knowledge about Egyptian culture; concerns about ethical sourcing; limited product selection; time constraints.

**Customer Gains**: confidence in authenticity; deeper cultural understanding; a feel-good purchase; a unique and meaningful souvenir; saved time and effort.

**Value Proposition** (mapped directly against the pains/gains above): unique authentic handicrafts (relieves "hard to find authentic," creates "confidence in authenticity"); storytelling and history (creates "deeper cultural understanding"); fair trade and ethical sourcing (relieves "concerns about ethical sourcing"); memorable souvenir and conversation starter (creates "unique and meaningful souvenir" gain); convenience and variety (relieves "limited selection" and "time constraints").

Notice every single Pain and Gain has a matching item in the Value Proposition column — that one-to-one mapping is what makes this a complete, fitted canvas rather than a wish list.

## Applying it for a client

Fill the Customer Profile side first and completely, using whatever market research is available (see [market-research-methodology.md](market-research-methodology.md)) — resist the urge to jump to the Value Proposition side before the Pains/Gains/Jobs are grounded in real customer input rather than founder assumption. Then, for every item on the Value Proposition side, require it to point at a specific Pain or Gain by name; anything that can't be pointed back is either cut or flagged as a feature the client is building for reasons other than customer need (compliance, cost, habit). This canvas is also the fastest way to spot when a client's product actually fits a *different* segment than the one they've been marketing to — if a feature relieves pains that don't appear on segment A's profile but would map perfectly to segment B, that's a targeting signal, not just a positioning one.

## Watch-outs

- Filling in the Value Proposition side from the founder's perspective ("what we're proud of building") instead of from validated customer research — produces a canvas that looks complete but doesn't actually predict purchase behavior.
- Treating "Jobs-to-be-done" as identical to "Pains" — Jobs are the underlying task the customer is trying to accomplish; Pains are what gets in their way while doing it. Conflating them loses the functional/emotional distinction that often reveals the real opportunity.
- A canvas with more Value Proposition items than matching Pains/Gains is a sign of feature bloat, not thoroughness — trim to what maps.
