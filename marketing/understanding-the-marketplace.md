# Understanding the Marketplace

The marketplace is the arena a business competes in — and before designing any strategy, you have to map what's actually in it: the conditions surrounding the business, the competitors fighting for the same customers, and the customers themselves. This is step 1 of the 5-step marketing process (see [intro-to-marketing.md](intro-to-marketing.md)) and the foundation everything else in the marketing curriculum sits on.

## When to use

At the start of any engagement, before a client commits to a positioning, a price, or a channel. Also revisit it whenever a market shifts (new entrant, new regulation, economic shock) — market understanding isn't a one-time exercise, it's step 1 of a cycle that repeats.

## How it works

**What is the market?** A market is a place where two parties gather to facilitate the exchange of goods and services. It can be:

- **Physical** — a store, a stall, a face-to-face exchange.
- **Virtual** — an e-commerce platform, an app, a digital marketplace.
- **Hybrid** — both at once (most real businesses today: a store with an online storefront, a service booked online but delivered in person).

**What can we find in the market?** Three buckets to research, each with its own toolkit:

| Bucket | What it covers | Tools |
|---|---|---|
| **Market Conditions** | The environment the business operates in — internal capability and external forces | SWOT Analysis, PESTEL Analysis |
| **Competitors** | Who else is fighting for the same customer | Competitor research, Market Size / Growth / Share |
| **Customers** | Who the business serves | Customer insights via market research |

(Full frameworks: [swot-analysis.md](swot-analysis.md), [pestel-analysis.md](pestel-analysis.md), [competitor-analysis-matrix.md](competitor-analysis-matrix.md), [market-research-methodology.md](market-research-methodology.md).)

**Three quantitative concepts to always ask about, for any market:**

1. **Market Size** (by value or volume) — the total value/quantity of all sales by all businesses in a market sector. Sets the ceiling on how big the opportunity is.
2. **Market Growth** — the percentage change in market size between two points in time (usually yearly). Tells you if the ceiling is rising or falling.
3. **Market Share** — the percentage of the total market held by one company or brand. Tells you how big a slice the client (or a competitor) currently holds, and how much room there is to take.

These three numbers together answer the question every client eventually asks: "is this market worth being in, and how much of it could we realistically get?"

## Example

A client wants to open a specialty tea shop in Cairo. Market type: hybrid (physical shop + delivery app presence). Market conditions: SWOT shows strength in sourcing relationships, weakness in brand awareness, opportunity in growing specialty-beverage interest among young professionals, threat from established café chains adding tea menus; PESTEL flags rising import taxes (Political/Economic) as a real cost risk. Market size: total specialty beverage retail spend in Cairo, estimated from industry reports (secondary data). Market growth: café sector has been growing double digits post-pandemic. Market share: currently zero (new entrant) — the real question becomes how much share is realistically capturable given the number of established competitors already splitting that market size.

## Applying it for a client

Start every new engagement with a one-page "marketplace snapshot": market type (physical/virtual/hybrid), a SWOT, a PESTEL scan (only the 1-2 factors per category that actually matter to this client — don't force all six), and a market size/growth/share estimate even if rough. This snapshot becomes the shared factual baseline the rest of the strategy (STP, 4Ps) gets built on, and it's the first thing to hand a client when they ask "why are we recommending this."

## 2024→2026 shift

Understanding the marketplace now includes a channel that didn't exist in the source material's framing: AI answer engines (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews) as a place customers research and get recommendations, alongside the physical/virtual/hybrid market types above.

- **Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) / Answer Engine Optimization (AEO)** — the emerging practice of optimizing a business's content and data so it gets *cited inside* an AI-generated answer, rather than optimizing for a ranked link on a traditional search-results page. The mechanics differ meaningfully from classic SEO: AI answer engines synthesize a single response from multiple sources and cite a handful of them, so the goal shifts from "rank #1" to "be one of the sources the model pulls from and credits" — which rewards clearly structured, factual, citable content (data, direct answers, well-labeled expertise) over content optimized purely for keyword ranking.
- **Why this matters for market sizing specifically:** Gartner has projected traditional search engine volume could drop by around 25% by 2026 as users shift queries to AI assistants and chat interfaces — a material change to how "Customers" (one of this note's three research buckets) actually discover a business, and a channel a client's competitor research and market-share estimate should now account for.

This is emerging-but-credible and the playbook is still being written: there's no mature, agreed-upon measurement standard yet for "AI answer share" the way there is for search rankings or market share, and the AI platforms' citation behavior keeps changing as the underlying models and retrieval methods evolve. Treat GEO/AEO as a real, worth-monitoring shift when scoping a client's marketplace — flag it in the Competitors and Customers buckets — but don't present a GEO recommendation with the same confidence as an SEO one; the evidence base is still thin and the tactics are moving targets.

## Watch-outs

- Market size/growth/share numbers are often unavailable or unreliable in emerging or informal markets (common in Egypt/MENA social-impact contexts) — triangulate from multiple secondary sources and be explicit about the confidence level, don't present a guess as a hard number.
- SWOT and PESTEL are easy to fill in generically ("weakness: limited budget") — push for specifics tied to this business, this market, this moment, or the analysis is worthless.
- "Understanding the marketplace" is a recurring input, not a one-off audit — competitors move, PESTEL factors shift (a new regulation, a currency shock), and market share estimates age quickly.
