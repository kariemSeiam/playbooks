# STP Model (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning)

STP is the three-step decision that answers "whom and how am I serving?" — it takes a segmented market, narrows it down to the segment(s) worth pursuing, and decides what image the brand should occupy in that target's mind. It's the strategic core that every other marketing tactic (pricing, channel, messaging) has to be consistent with.

## When to use

Right after market segmentation is done (see [market-segmentation-customer-vs-buyer.md](market-segmentation-customer-vs-buyer.md)) and before touching the 4Ps (see [marketing-mix-4ps.md](marketing-mix-4ps.md)). Also revisit STP whenever a business's growth stalls, a new competitor enters, or a client can't answer "who is this for, in one sentence."

## How it works

**1. Segmentation** — already covered in [market-segmentation-customer-vs-buyer.md](market-segmentation-customer-vs-buyer.md) (Geographic, Demographic, Psychographic, Behavioral variables). STP consumes that output as its starting material.

**2. Targeting** — choosing which segment(s) to actually pursue, evaluated against criteria:

- **Size** — the target segment needs to be large enough to justify the marketing effort spent on it.
- **Profitability** — it should bring higher revenues and profits than the cost of marketing to it.
- **Difference** — segments should be genuinely differentiated from each other, and their targeting/messaging differentiated accordingly (if two segments would get the same treatment, they're not really separate segments for strategy purposes).
- **Adaptability** — different segments require different benefits emphasized; the offer needs to flex.
- **Accessibility** — assess how easy it is to actually acquire a new customer from this segment (can you reach them, can you convert them) — a large, profitable, distinct segment you can't reach isn't a usable target.

**Targeting strategies** (how many segments to go after, and how):

1. **Undifferentiated Strategy** — one offer for the whole market, ignoring segment differences (mass marketing).
2. **Single Segment Strategy (Niche Targeting)** — focus on one specific segment exclusively.
3. **Multi-segment Strategy** — pursue several segments, typically with a distinct offer/message per segment.

**3. Positioning** — for the brand to occupy the right image in the mind of the target customer, relative to alternatives. Three types (often combined):

- **Functional Positioning** — solves a problem.
- **Symbolic Positioning** — fulfills the self-image, ego, or belongingness need of the customer.
- **Experiential Positioning** — highlights the elements that connect emotionally with the customer.

**Differentiation** underpins positioning: what is the brand's Unique Selling Proposition (USP), and what elements were considered in defining it (this is where the Competitor Analysis Matrix's "Differentiators" row and the Value Proposition Canvas both feed directly in — see [competitor-analysis-matrix.md](competitor-analysis-matrix.md) and [value-proposition-canvas.md](value-proposition-canvas.md)).

**Positioning statement template** — the fill-in-the-blank tool to force a concrete, single-sentence position:

> To *(target group and need)*, *(Brand)* is the brand of *(competitive framework)* that *(benefit)*. The reason is *(reason why)*. The brand character is *(brand character)*.

## Example — Handicraft Company in Egypt (worked case from the source material)

**Customer Segments**: Primary — American tourists aged 30-50 with an interest in traditional Egyptian art and culture, visiting Luxor for the first time. Secondary — European tourists with similar interests visiting major tourist destinations in Egypt. Potential — online buyers interested in authentic Egyptian handicrafts.

Targeting this via the criteria above: the primary segment (American first-time Luxor tourists) is sized by tourism-arrival data, profitable given tourists' willingness to pay for authentic souvenirs, clearly different in need from the online-buyer segment (in-person browsing vs. e-commerce trust signals), and accessible through hotel/tour-operator partnerships. That justifies a **multi-segment strategy** — primary in-market tourists served differently from the potential online-buyer segment.

**Positioning**, filled into the template: *"To American and European tourists seeking authentic cultural souvenirs, [Brand] is the brand of Egyptian handicrafts that guarantees fair-trade, ethically-sourced, storytelling-rich pieces you won't find in mass-market souvenir shops. The reason is our direct partnerships with local artisans. The brand character is warm, trustworthy, and culturally proud."* This combines Functional positioning (solves "hard to find authentic souvenirs"), Symbolic positioning (being the tourist who bought something real, not tourist-trap kitsch), and Experiential positioning (storytelling and history as part of the purchase).

## Applying it for a client

Work STP in strict order and don't let a client jump to positioning language before targeting is settled — a beautiful brand story aimed at the wrong segment is wasted work. Use the fill-in-the-blank positioning statement literally in workshops; forcing a client to complete every blank (especially "the reason why" and "competitive framework") exposes vague thinking faster than an open-ended positioning discussion does. Once the sentence is complete and the client can say it without hedging, that's the brief every downstream 4Ps decision gets checked against.

## Watch-outs

- Choosing a target segment based on what's easiest to imagine (often the founder's own demographic) rather than testing it against all five targeting criteria, especially Accessibility — an appealing segment you can't actually reach isn't a real target.
- Positioning statements that could apply to any competitor in the category ("we offer quality and great service") — if a competitor's name could be substituted into the same sentence without it sounding wrong, the positioning hasn't actually differentiated anything.
- Multi-segment strategies multiply marketing cost and message complexity — confirm the business actually has the resources to serve more than one segment distinctly before recommending it over a single-segment focus.
