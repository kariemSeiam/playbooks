# How Brands Grow (Evidence-Based Brand Growth)

A large-scale empirical challenge to conventional brand strategy, led by Byron Sharp and the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute (University of South Australia) using decades of purchase-panel data across categories and countries. Its central claim: brands grow primarily by increasing **penetration** — the number of different people who buy them, even lightly or occasionally — not by deepening loyalty among a small existing base or by chasing "meaningful differentiation." It's the reality check against brand strategy that over-invests in existing-customer loyalty programs and under-invests in reaching the light and non-buyers who represent most of the growth opportunity in almost every category.

## When to use

- A client wants revenue growth and defaults straight to "let's build loyalty," when the data actually shows most category growth comes from acquiring new or light buyers, not deepening existing ones
- Media and reach planning — deciding how broad versus narrow a targeting strategy should be
- Challenging a "we need to be different, not better" positioning brief before it's finalized
- Evaluating whether a brand's growth problem is really a distribution or availability problem in disguise, not a messaging problem
- Explaining why heavy segmentation or niche-targeting strategies often cap a brand's growth ceiling

## How it works

**Four core empirical laws, each backed by cross-category panel data:**

1. **The Law of Double Jeopardy.** Smaller brands have both fewer buyers (lower penetration) *and* those buyers are slightly less loyal on average (lower purchase frequency). This is a double penalty, not a single one: small brands don't have a hidden pool of super-loyal fans to fall back on — their light-buyer base is genuinely less loyal, purely as a mathematical consequence of being smaller in the category. This directly undercuts strategies built on "we have a small but fiercely loyal base."

2. **Penetration drives growth far more than loyalty does.** Across the categories studied, brand growth overwhelmingly correlates with more people buying at least once, not with existing buyers buying more often. Loyalty metrics (share of wallet, repeat rate) tend to be remarkably stable across brands of different sizes within a category — per Double Jeopardy, they aren't the lever most brands actually have room to pull.

3. **Mental and Physical Availability drive penetration.**
   - **Mental Availability** — the propensity of a brand to come to mind in buying situations, built through broad, distinctive, continuous advertising reach — not narrow, deep targeting — that refreshes Distinctive Brand Assets across as many buying situations as possible.
   - **Physical Availability** — being easy to find and buy, in as many relevant channels as possible, with minimal friction. A brand that's loved but hard to buy loses to a brand that's merely known and always available.

4. **Distinctive Assets over Differentiation.** Sharp's data suggests most brands in a mature category are not meaningfully differentiated on functional benefits — what separates them at the point of purchase is **distinctiveness**: colors, logos, taglines, characters, sonic identity, packaging shape that let a buyer identify the brand faster than competitors, without the brand needing to be functionally different. Distinctive assets protect and build Mental Availability by being unmistakably linked to the brand and used with extreme consistency over years.

**The practical implication.** Since most category buyers are light, infrequent buyers of any given brand (not heavy loyalists), and Double Jeopardy means small brands can't out-loyalize their way to growth, the practical strategy is: reach as many category buyers as possible with broad, not narrow, targeting; be present and easy to buy everywhere they might look; and be instantly recognizable via consistent distinctive assets — rather than crafting a narrow, differentiated positioning for a tightly segmented niche.

## Example

A regional cola brand versus a global leader, viewed through this lens: both brands' loyal buyers behave similarly in purchase frequency (Double Jeopardy predicts the smaller brand's buyers are only slightly less frequent, not radically less loyal) — the real gap is penetration, since far more people buy the global brand at least occasionally. That brand's advantage isn't a superior "meaningfully different" taste story — blind-taste data across the cola category repeatedly shows weak functional differentiation — it's Mental Availability, built through decades of broad-reach advertising across countless occasions and a small set of highly consistent Distinctive Assets (the script wordmark, the color, the bottle contour), plus near-total Physical Availability in virtually every retail channel, fridge, and menu. A consultant advising the smaller brand, using this model, would prioritize broadening distribution and locking down one or two consistently used distinctive assets over crafting a differentiated flavor-story campaign aimed at a narrow "cola connoisseur" segment.

## Applying it for a client

Before signing off on a "differentiated positioning for our core loyal segment" strategy, check the numbers if the client has any purchase data: is the growth opportunity really in the existing loyal base (usually small and already saturated), or in the much larger pool of light and non-buyers of the category who aren't being reached at all? For most clients outside genuinely novel or niche categories, redirect budget from loyalty-program depth toward two concrete audits: whether the product is actually easy to find and buy everywhere the category is bought, online and offline (Physical Availability), and whether the brand has one to three consistently used, ownable identifiers rather than a new campaign concept every quarter (Distinctive Assets). This is also the sharpest tool for pushing back on an agency-driven "let's find our meaningfully different positioning" brief that isn't grounded in evidence the category actually rewards functional differentiation.

## Watch-outs

- This model describes mass-market, established, repeat-purchase categories (FMCG, telecom, banking, retail) with good panel data behind it — it travels less cleanly to genuinely novel categories, B2B with few buyers, or markets where differentiation is a real, defensible moat; apply it as a default prior, not a universal law, and check it against [stp-model.md](../marketing/stp-model.md) positioning work rather than discarding positioning outright.
- "Distinctive, not different" is frequently misread by clients as "we don't need to be good" — the model doesn't argue quality doesn't matter (see the Performance block in [brand-equity-keller-cbbe.md](../marketing/brand-equity-keller-cbbe.md)), only that differentiation specifically is overrated relative to distinctiveness and availability as growth levers.
- Broad-reach, mental-availability-first media strategy requires sustained budget over years — it's a poor fit for a client that needs a short-term sales spike, where narrower, more targeted tactics may still outperform.
- Sharp's data is aggregate, cross-category evidence, not a guarantee for any single client's market — validate the Double Jeopardy pattern against the client's own purchase data before restructuring their strategy around it, especially where reliable panel data is scarce (see the similar watch-out in [understanding-the-marketplace.md](../marketing/understanding-the-marketplace.md)).
