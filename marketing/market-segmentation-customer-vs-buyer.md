# Market Segmentation & Customer vs. Buyer

Market segmentation is the act of splitting a broad market into groups of consumers who share meaningful traits, so a business can tailor products and branding to a specific group instead of trying to be everything to everyone. A companion idea that trips up almost every founder the first time: the person who *uses* the product (the customer) is not always the person who *pays* for it (the buyer) — and marketing has to address both.

## When to use

Immediately after mapping the marketplace (see [understanding-the-marketplace.md](understanding-the-marketplace.md)) and before choosing a target segment in STP (see [stp-model.md](stp-model.md)) — segmentation is the raw material STP's "S" step consumes. Also use the customer-vs-buyer lens any time a product is bought by someone other than the end user (kids' products, gifts, B2B procurement, healthcare, donor-funded / social-impact programs).

## How it works

**Why segment at all** — three concrete payoffs: identify targeted groups of consumers so products and branding can be tailored in a way that's attractive to that specific group; minimize risk by figuring out which products are most likely to earn a share of a given target market; focus resources on the segments and efforts most likely to be profitable, instead of spreading thin across everyone.

**The four segmentation variable types** (the standard toolkit — mix and match, don't feel obligated to use all four for every business):

| Type | Variables |
|---|---|
| **Geographic** | Region, country size, city size, population density, climate |
| **Demographic** | Age, gender, family size, income, occupation, education, race, nationality |
| **Psychographic** | Social class, lifestyle, personality |
| **Behavioral** | Purchase occasion, user status, usage rate, loyalty status, attitude towards product |

Demographic and geographic variables are the easiest to gather (often already in secondary data) but the weakest predictors of behavior on their own; psychographic and behavioral variables predict actual purchase decisions better but require primary research (see [market-research-methodology.md](market-research-methodology.md)) to uncover.

**Customer vs. Buyer.** The question to always ask: *is the customer necessarily the buyer?* Frequently, no — a toy is used by a child but bought by a parent; a hospital's patient is the customer but an insurer or the government may be the buyer; a donor-funded program's beneficiary is the customer but the donor is the buyer. Key message: market segmentation helps tailor products and services to specific segments per their needs, which expands market share and outreach — but only if you've correctly identified *whose* needs (the user's) and *whose* purchase decision (the payer's) you're actually segmenting and marketing to, because they can require entirely different messages.

## Example

A children's educational-tablet startup segments geographically (urban Egyptian governorates), demographically (households with children aged 5-10, middle-to-upper income), psychographically (parents who value structured learning over free play), and behaviorally (first-time tech buyers for their child vs. upgraders). The customer is the child (uses the product, cares about fun and ease); the buyer is the parent (pays, cares about educational value and safety). The product design speaks to the child; the marketing message and pricing justification speak to the parent — two different communication strategies for one purchase decision.

## Applying it for a client

Run segmentation as a two-pass exercise: first pass, brainstorm every plausible segment across all four variable types without filtering; second pass, for each segment ask "who's the customer here, and who's the buyer — are they the same person?" That second question alone often reveals that a client's messaging has been aimed at the wrong party (e.g., a B2B SaaS founder writing marketing copy for the end-user employee when the actual buyer is a procurement manager who cares about entirely different things). Feed the resulting segment list directly into the Targeting step of STP.

## Watch-outs

- Segmenting only on demographic/geographic data because it's the easiest to get, while skipping psychographic/behavioral data that would actually explain *why* people buy — the trap of segmenting on what's measurable rather than what's meaningful.
- Forgetting the buyer entirely when the customer is unambiguous to the founder ("obviously the parent is the buyer") — worth stating explicitly even when it seems obvious, because it changes which channel and message wins the purchase decision.
- Over-segmenting into dozens of micro-segments with no resources to serve any of them distinctly — segmentation is only useful up to the point a business can actually act on the distinction (see Targeting criteria in [stp-model.md](stp-model.md)).
