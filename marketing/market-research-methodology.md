# Market Research Methodology

Market research is the disciplined process of gathering, analyzing, and interpreting information about a market, a product or service, and past/present/potential customers — their characteristics, spending habits, location, needs, the industry as a whole, and the specific competitors faced. It's the engine that turns "we think" into "we know" before a client commits money to a strategy.

## When to use

Continuously — not as a one-off gate before launch. Use it at every stage of the product life cycle, with a different emphasis each time:

- **Development**: ideation, product testing.
- **Introduction**: competitive analysis, consumer segmentation, pricing research.
- **Growth**: customer-experience evaluation, satisfaction measurement.
- **Maturity**: innovation research, gap analysis.

The headline point: market research runs **all the time**, not just at kickoff. Reach for it specifically whenever a client is about to: set a price, launch or reposition a product, enter a new segment, or is confused about why customers behave the way they do.

**Why it's worth the cost** — it gives an accurate view of the business and marketplace; identifies potential and problem areas; determines who and where customers are and their needs/preferences; reveals how customers view the business versus competitors; identifies new business opportunities and changing trends; recognizes new areas for expansion; discovers potential customers and their needs; sets achievable growth/sales targets; and supports well-informed strategic decisions. In short: it replaces founder intuition with evidence at exactly the points where intuition is most likely to be wrong (pricing, positioning, and unmet needs).

## How it works

**Step 1 — The Methodology.** Before collecting anything, define the process that will get you from a question to actionable insight. Two starting artifacts:

- **Research Question** — open, exploratory framing: *"What are the needs of graduating high school students in identifying their field of study?"* / *"What is the optimal price range for a new national sportswear brand?"* / *"How accepting are people of recycled clothes, and who is the target segment?"*
- **Hypothesis** — a specific, testable claim to confirm or reject: *"There is a gap in the affordable trustworthy home-nursing sector in Egypt."* / *"Housewives struggle to market their handmade products."* / *"The online shopping market lacks a system that applies quality checks and consumer specifications."*

Research Questions are for early, exploratory situations (usually resolved with qualitative research); Hypotheses are for situations where you already have a specific claim to validate (usually resolved with quantitative research).

**Step 2 — Type of Data.**

| | Secondary Data ("desk research") | Primary Data ("field research") |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Data already compiled and organized by someone else | New, first-hand data not previously collected |
| Sources | Reports, government-funded studies, textbooks, historical records, statistical databases (Google advanced search, Google news archive, CAPMAS, published research) | You — direct collection from the market |
| Use it when | The question has likely already been answered by someone (market size, demographics, industry trends) | The question is specific to this business/customer and no one else has asked it |

Always check secondary data first — it's faster and cheaper — and only move to primary research for what secondary sources can't answer.

**Step 3 — Type of Research: Qualitative vs. Quantitative.**

| | Qualitative Research | Quantitative Research |
|---|---|---|
| Answers | What? How? Why? | How many? Who all? |
| Purpose | Diagnoses underlying consumer needs, motivations, preferences, and behavior; used to *develop* hypotheses; adds richness to quantitative findings | Used to *validate* hypotheses; provides projectable, statistically rigorous results; sets benchmarks and supports decisions |
| Method character | Small, carefully selected number of individuals — enough to make directional hypotheses with conviction; attentive to verbal (spontaneous spoken responses) and non-verbal signals (body language, silences, posture) and group dynamics; does not claim statistical validity but is objective and reliable | Structured questions asked of a large, scientifically-drawn sample representative of the population, to get facts and statistics rather than impressions |

The two are sequential partners, not competitors: qualitative research is typically run *first* to figure out what to even ask and to generate hypotheses; quantitative research is run *after* to test those hypotheses at scale and get numbers a business can act on with confidence.

**Qualitative research techniques:**

- **Focus Group Discussions (FGD)** — see full breakdown below.
- **In-depth Interviews (IDI)** — one-on-one, deep exploration of an individual's experience/motivation, where FGD's group dynamic would suppress candor (sensitive topics, competitive B2B contexts).
- **Ethnographic Research** — observing behavior in its natural context rather than asking about it, useful when stated preference and actual behavior diverge.

**Focus Group Discussions (FGD) in depth:**

- **What it is**: a discussion with a group of people — typically one moderator plus 4-10 participants. Sub-formats: Mini Groups (4-6 participants) and Focus Groups proper (7-10 participants).
- **When to use it**: to explore behavior, attitudes, and processes; to address "why," "what," and "how" questions; on topics that are *not* sensitive (sensitive topics push toward IDI instead, where people won't self-censor in front of a group); when you want breadth of reaction rather than depth on any one person.
- **How it works**: define the target audience; identify respondents who fit a particular "spec" (screening criteria); sessions last 1-3 hours; a moderator guides the discussion; it takes place in a facility where reactions can be observed (in person or via video).
- **Example use cases**: deciding what type of product to launch in a new market, evaluating concepts for new product development, evaluating concepts for new ad campaigns, gauging attitudes to public health or community issues, testing new banking products/services, understanding shopper habits and category decision-making.

**How to use qualitative data once you have it** (data analysis turns raw transcripts into usable information that supports interpretation — enabling comparisons between groups/sub-groups, ranking important results, identifying similarities/differences between responses, and spotting trends):

1. Design a discussion guide (before fieldwork).
2. Organize a data collection tool (notes template, recording, coding sheet).
3. Draw themes from the raw data.
4. Interpret themes and link them back to sub-groups (does this theme hold for everyone, or only for one segment?).
5. Draw conclusions and an action plan — the step most often skipped; without it, the FGD produced an interesting conversation and no decision.

**Quantitative research in practice.** Means asking people for their opinions, habits, and preferences in a *structured* way, so the results are facts and statistics rather than impressions. To get reliable results, survey a fairly large number of people, and make sure they're a representative sample of the target market (draw a sample from the full population, don't just survey whoever is convenient). The quantitative cycle runs: **Survey Questions → Survey Design → Survey Distribution → Survey Collection → Survey Analysis** (and back to refining questions for the next round).

**Data-collection channels** (the deck lists these under fieldwork technique — they apply mainly to structured/quantitative collection, though some overlap with qualitative fieldwork too): door-to-door, street intercept, CATI/telephonic interviews, Central Location Testing (CLT), mystery shopping/mystery calling, home placement and recall, exit interviews, shopper/aisle interviews, market scanning and in-store observation, online research.

**Overall research-methodology checklist** — six things to nail down before fielding *any* study, qualitative or quantitative:

1. **Customer** — know your customer, know your population (who is the universe you're trying to learn about).
2. **Sampling** — who will you actually survey/interview out of that population?
3. **Data collection tool** — what are your unknowns/questions, precisely?
4. **Data collection methods** — which channel is most convenient given the above?
5. **Field logistics** — what techniques and operational steps will you follow to actually gather it?
6. **Data analysis** — how will the raw data turn into actionable insight?
And always weigh this against **time, cost, and resources** — research can be expensive, and the "right" method on paper is sometimes the wrong one given the team's actual capacity.

**"Moving forward" — getting started on research for a specific client (6 steps):**

1. Identify your market — who are the competitors, what variety do they offer, what's their edge?
2. Identify your target customer — who is the consumer, why would they choose you, are they potential (i.e. reachable) customers?
3. Understand your gap areas — where are you in the business life cycle, and what specifically do you need to research?
4. Determine your research capacity — research can be costly; do you have a team, what's their capacity, can you get external help?
5. Choose the most convenient method — which type of research and why; start early and leave a margin of flexibility.
6. Conduct meaningful research — focus on the data you actually need; gather meaningful data for the business, not data for its own sake.

## Example

A founder hypothesizes: "Housewives struggle to market their handmade products." Step 1 (methodology): this is a hypothesis, not an open question — it needs validating, not exploring from scratch, though a short qualitative pass first would sharpen it. Step 2 (data): check secondary sources first (any existing reports on Egyptian informal-economy handicraft sellers, e-commerce penetration data) before fielding anything. Step 3 (research type): run 2-3 small FGDs with housewives who sell handmade goods to understand *why* and *how* they struggle (qualitative, develops the hypothesis into specifics — is it pricing knowledge, photography, platform access, time?) — then follow with a quantitative survey of a larger, representative sample to find out *how many* struggle with each specific barrier identified, so the eventual product/service can be sized and prioritized correctly.

## Applying it for a client

Never let a client skip straight to a survey (quantitative) because "we need numbers for the pitch deck" — if the underlying questions and hypotheses haven't been sharpened by qualitative work first, the survey will ask the wrong questions precisely and confidently. Sequence it: cheap secondary research first, then a small qualitative pass to sharpen the hypothesis and the questions, then quantitative research to validate at scale and produce the numbers stakeholders want. Budget and calendar time for all three phases up front, since "determine your research capacity" is explicitly one of the six moving-forward steps — clients routinely underestimate cost and timeline for primary research.

## Watch-outs

- Treating FGD output as if it were statistically representative ("60% of our focus group said X") — qualitative research is explicitly *not* meant to claim statistical validity; report it as directional themes, not percentages.
- Running FGDs on genuinely sensitive topics (health, income, family conflict) where group dynamics will suppress honest answers — switch to In-Depth Interviews instead.
- Skipping step 4/5 of "how to use qualitative data" (interpret themes, draw conclusions and an action plan) — a transcript full of quotes with no synthesized decision is not a finished research output.
- Surveying a convenient sample instead of a representative one for quantitative work — invalidates the "projectable results" that are quantitative research's whole value proposition.
