# Break-Even Analysis & Return on Investment

Break-even analysis tells a business the exact sales volume (or revenue) at which it stops losing money — the point where revenue equals total costs and profit is zero. ROI tells you, after the fact, whether money already spent on something (a product, a campaign, a piece of equipment) earned back more than it cost. Together they answer the two questions every early-stage owner actually needs answered: "how much do I have to sell before I'm not losing money?" and "was that spend worth it?"

## When to use

- **Break-even:** before setting a price, before deciding whether a new product/branch/service line is viable, before telling an investor what "minimum viable sales" looks like, whenever fixed or variable costs change materially.
- **ROI:** after (or before, as a projection) any discrete spend with an identifiable cost and identifiable return — a marketing campaign, new equipment, a new hire, a whole new business versus leaving the money in a bank deposit.
- **Financial performance:** as a periodic (monthly/quarterly) health check that pulls together budget, income statement, cash flow, and equity into one comparative view — useful when benchmarking a client against competitors or against their own prior period.

## How it works

### Break-even point

Break-even = the level of activity where a company's revenues and expenses are exactly equal — no net profit, no net loss. It answers: how much revenue must be reached to cover both fixed and variable costs for a given period?

Rule of thumb: **the higher the break-even point, the greater the risk the company carries; the lower it is, the lower the risk.**

**Formula (units):**

```
Break-even (units) = Fixed costs / (Unit price − Variable cost per unit)
```

- **Fixed costs** — costs that do NOT move with sales volume (e.g. rent).
- **Variable costs** — costs that DO move with sales volume (e.g. raw materials per unit produced).
- **Unit price − Variable cost per unit** = the **contribution margin** — how much of each unit sold is left over to pay down fixed costs (and, above break-even, become profit).

**Formula (value / revenue):**

```
Break-even (value) = Fixed costs / Contribution margin ratio
Contribution margin ratio = (Unit price − Variable cost per unit) / Unit price
```

Sanity check: break-even value always equals break-even units × unit price — use this to catch arithmetic errors, since the ratio form is easy to invert by mistake.

**Why it matters (7 uses):**
1. Gives a benchmark for building long-term business plans.
2. Helps price products and set minimum sales targets, feeding sales goals and pricing strategy.
3. Shows how much different strategies (pricing, cost-cutting, volume) move business performance.
4. Tells investors the exact point past which profit begins, so they can size the upside they're chasing.
5. Is a standard, expected component of any business or investment plan.
6. Lets you pressure-test a new product line before launch, since a new product brings its own new fixed + variable costs.
7. Tells you your loss exposure if sales fall short.

**Factors that push break-even up:**
- Rising customer demand that forces more production (and therefore more production expense) to keep up.
- Rising variable costs (materials, rent, salaries) while demand/price stay flat.
- Equipment failure or a faltering production line — output drops below what's needed in the period, and repair itself adds cost.

**Factors that pull break-even down:**
- Raising the unit price (owners often resist this, fearing customer loss — but it directly lowers the units needed to break even).
- Cutting production/manufacturing costs, which raises profit at the same volume.

### Financial performance

Financial performance is the measure of how well a company uses its core-business assets to generate revenue — its overall financial health over a period, usable to benchmark against similar companies or industries. It is read off four basic financial statements:

| Statement | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Balance sheet (budget) | Value of assets and liabilities |
| Income statement | Results of operations over a period (see [income-statement-vs-balance-sheet.md](income-statement-vs-balance-sheet.md)) |
| Statement of cash flows | Operating, investing, and financing activity (see [cash-flow.md](cash-flow.md)) |
| Statement of shareholders' equity | Cumulative shareholder rights, capital, and retained earnings |

Ways to improve it: capture available government grants, arrange/renegotiate expenses, recover outstanding debt, offer multiple payment options to customers, put unused assets to work, and invest surplus rather than let it sit idle. The underlying discipline is the same as budgeting: track expenses constantly, get correct invoices, and cut spend wherever possible — so you always know whether you're doing well or not, rather than finding out at year-end.

### Return on Investment (ROI)

ROI measures the return an investment generated relative to what it cost — a performance ratio used both to judge one investment and to compare the relative efficiency of several. A high ROI means the gain compares favorably to the cost.

**Calculating it — two steps:**
1. **Total the investment's costs**: initial payment, direct and indirect costs incurred, ongoing maintenance costs, and any taxes/fees tied to the investment. All of these must be captured, or the ROI number will be wrong.
2. **Total the income/profit the investment produced**, over the period the profit was actually realized in.

**Formula:**

```
ROI = Net profit / Total investment costs × 100
ROI = (Total investment income − Total investment costs) / Total investment costs
```

## Example

**Break-even (deck's own numbers):** Fixed costs = 50,000 EGP. Variable cost per unit = 2 EGP. Selling price per unit = 6 EGP.

```
Break-even (units) = 50,000 / (6 − 2) = 12,500 units
Break-even (value)  = 12,500 units × 6 EGP = 75,000 EGP
                    = 50,000 / ((6−2)/6) = 50,000 / 0.667 = 75,000 EGP
```

The company must manufacture and sell 12,500 units (75,000 EGP of revenue) to cover all fixed and variable costs — at that point profit is exactly zero; every unit past it is profit.

**ROI (deck's own numbers):** Spend 1,000 EGP/month on electronic advertising; that spend generates 1,500 EGP in sales revenue.

```
ROI = (1,500 − 1,000) / 1,000 = 50%
```

For every pound spent on the campaign, the investor gets back 0.5 EGP in net return on top of the pound spent (i.e. 1.5x total return).

**ROI decision case (deck's own activity, solved):** A founder needs 30,000 EGP to launch a small business and expects 36,000 EGP profit in year one. The alternative is depositing the same 30,000 EGP in a bank at 15% annual interest.

```
Business ROI = (36,000 − 30,000) / 30,000 = 20%
Bank return  = 30,000 × 15% = 4,500 EGP → ROI = 15%
```

20% > 15%, so — on a pure return basis — launching the business is the better financial decision. (In reality you'd also weight risk, liquidity, and time invested, which the bank deposit doesn't require — see Watch-outs.)

## Applying it for a client

Run break-even before every pricing conversation, not after. Clients routinely price a product off "what feels right" or "what competitors charge" without ever checking whether that price clears their own cost structure — get the fixed/variable split first (this is the same split you build during budgeting), then reverse-engineer: at this price, how many units do we need to sell monthly, and is that number realistic given their actual market/channel capacity? If the break-even unit count is implausible, the fix is price, cost, or both — not "sell harder." For ROI, use it to force a real decision between competing uses of the same capital (new hire vs. new equipment vs. more ad spend vs. just paying down debt) rather than letting the client fund whatever they're most excited about. Always frame ROI next to the client's real opportunity cost (a bank rate, a competitor's return, or "what if we just didn't spend it") — a standalone ROI% means little without that comparison.

## Watch-outs

- Break-even assumes costs cleanly split into "fixed" and "variable" — many real costs (part-time labor, tiered supplier pricing) are semi-variable; force a judgment call rather than skipping the analysis.
- Break-even is a single-product, single-price model by default; a multi-product business needs a blended/weighted break-even or one per product line.
- A rising break-even point isn't automatically bad — it can simply mean the business scaled production to meet real demand (read the *cause*, not just the number).
- ROI ignores time and risk unless you deliberately account for them: a 20% ROI over 3 years is very different from 20% over 3 months, and a risky venture's ROI isn't directly comparable to a guaranteed bank rate without a risk adjustment.
- ROI also ignores cash-flow timing — a profitable investment on paper can still bankrupt a business if the cash arrives too late (cross-check against [cash-flow.md](cash-flow.md)).
