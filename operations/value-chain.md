# Value Chain

Michael Porter's value chain is a model that lays out every activity a business performs to take a product or service from raw idea to delivered, paid-for output — purchasing, producing, marketing, distributing, and servicing it. Its point isn't just to list activities; it's to find, step by step, where the business adds real value at the lowest possible cost, because that gap between value created and cost incurred (the *margin*) is where competitive advantage actually lives.

## When to use

- Diagnosing where a client's operation is bleeding value or cost, activity by activity, rather than guessing at "the business" as one undifferentiated blob.
- Deciding where to invest scarce resources: the value chain forces you to ask, for each activity, "does this add value the customer will pay for, or is it a cost we're carrying without a matching benefit?"
- Structuring a resourcing conversation with an early-stage founder — mapping their few employees, tools, and cash against primary vs. support activities usually reveals gaps nobody had named yet (e.g. no one owns procurement, or "marketing" and "sales" are being run by the same overloaded person as an afterthought).
- Setting up ongoing operational discipline (performance measurement, staying current, formalizing procedures) once the chain itself is mapped.

## How it works

### The two-part structure

Porter's diagram splits every activity in the business into two bands:

- **Primary activities** (drawn lengthwise, "activities or implementation") — the activities directly involved in creating, selling, and delivering the product:
  1. **Inbound logistics** — receiving, warehousing, and allocating incoming raw materials and inventory.
  2. **Operations** — manufacturing, equipment management, assembly: turning inputs into the finished product.
  3. **Outbound logistics** — order fulfillment, shipping, finished-goods storage: getting the product to the customer.
  4. **Marketing and sales** — advertising, branding, quoting: getting the customer to want and buy the product.
  5. **Service** — customer-focused repairs, warranties, returns: what happens after the sale.

- **Support activities** (drawn crosswise, "equipment or resources") — the activities that make the primary activities possible:
  1. **Infrastructure** — the company's own systems: planning, financing, accounting.
  2. **Technological development** — research and development.
  3. **Human resource management** — hiring and retention.
  4. **Procurement** — sourcing raw materials.

Between the two bands sits the **margin** — the value delivered to the customer minus what it cost across every activity to deliver it. Value chain analysis exists to grow that margin: by evaluating detailed procedures at each step, the company finds where it can raise value delivered, cut cost incurred, or both — which is also how it tells apart the activities that build competitive advantage from the ones that just create waste and drag on market leadership.

### The same activities, described operationally

It helps to also hold a plainer, more operational restatement of the primary activities — useful when talking to a founder who has never seen Porter's diagram:

- **Sourcing (inbound logistics)** — contracting with vendors to purchase goods, raw materials, or whatever the operation needs.
- **Transport, storage, loading, unloading** — the physical handling of supply once it arrives; production/manufacturing businesses give this heavy attention, while commercially-driven businesses shift more of their attention downstream, toward marketing and after-sales.
- **Production and distribution (operations + outbound logistics)** — the product or service moving through internal production and then through distribution channels until it reaches the customer.
- **Marketing, promotion, advertising, and selling** — building the offer, researching the market, analyzing competitors, and developing the customer relationships that pull the customer in.
- **After-sales service** — everything that sustains satisfaction after the purchase: service, maintenance, repair.

### Support processes, restated for a small operation

For a small or early-stage business, the four support activities translate into four concrete resource pools that must be planned for up front:

- **Machines, devices, and assets** — bought and paid for once, then used repeatedly; the value they return compounds over time.
- **Human resources** — the team itself: the skills, knowledge, and prior experience they bring. Most of the value a small operation adds on top of its raw inputs comes from its people, not its equipment.
- **Equipment / infrastructure** — the utilities a project cannot function without: electricity, water, internet, computers. Some of these (electricity, water) are infrastructure in the classic sense; others (a website, digital marketing tooling) depend on the same base infrastructure to exist at all. Without it, the project stops.
- **Financial resources** — liquidity/cash on hand, which is what actually lets the business acquire the other three: place, rent, equipment, human resources, assets.

### The operational view: keeping the chain running

Once the chain is mapped, three ongoing disciplines keep it functioning rather than decaying into a one-time diagram:

- **Performance measurement** — starts with goal-setting: management sets clear, achievable targets, then tracks a system that shows progress against them. Without a tracking system, "performance measurement" is just an opinion.
- **Keeping up with developments** — staying aware of what's happening across the sector: new innovations, new methods, what competitors are doing — so the business can out-innovate rather than fall behind.
- **Organizing the procedures followed** — actively looking for new tools, software, and equipment that make existing procedures faster or cheaper. The deck's own example: a company running inventory manually can adopt inventory software and save significant time and effort — the win isn't inventing a new process, it's removing friction from the one that already works.

## Example

A small mushroom-growing operation (used elsewhere in this program as a running example — see [asset-types.md](asset-types.md) and [procurement-and-logistics.md](procurement-and-logistics.md)) maps onto the chain like this: **inbound logistics** = sourcing spawn, rice straw, and sterilization supplies; **operations** = the sterilization → inoculation → incubation → harvest cycle; **outbound logistics** = packing harvested mushrooms into branded cartons and transporting them, same-day, straight to the buying factories (no retail storefront); **marketing and sales** = direct outreach to the handful of factories and companies that buy the crop; **service** = whatever quality/consistency guarantees keep those factory relationships intact. Support activities are correspondingly thin: infrastructure is the shed and irrigation/cooling system, HR is a small operating team, procurement is the recurring monthly raw-material buy, and technological development is close to zero at this scale — which is itself diagnostic: an early-stage operation this small should not be trying to fund R&D it can't afford yet.

## Applying it for a client

Draw the chain with the client, activity by activity, and for each one ask two questions: *who owns this today* (often "no one, it just happens") and *does the customer actually pay for this activity's output, or are we assuming they do*. Early-stage clients routinely over-invest in one primary activity (usually operations — building the product) while leaving another skeletal (usually marketing/sales, or after-sales service) because the founder's own skills sit on one side of the chain. Naming this explicitly, with the diagram in front of them, is often more persuasive than telling them "you need to hire a salesperson." On the support side, use the four-resource restatement (assets, HR, equipment/infrastructure, financial resources) as a startup checklist: a client who's excited about the product but hasn't priced out their infrastructure or financial-resource runway has skipped a support activity that will stall the primary ones. Finally, push every client toward at least the performance-measurement discipline — even a single tracked KPI per primary activity turns the value chain from a slide into an operating tool.

## Watch-outs

- The value chain is a diagnostic lens, not a literal org chart — a two-person startup still has all nine activities happening, just consolidated onto fewer people; don't let a client conclude "we don't have a marketing function" when what they mean is "one person does it badly, unnoticed."
- Don't over-weight technological development for early-stage clients — Porter designed this model for mature, often large, companies; a pre-seed founder's "R&D" support activity may legitimately be near-zero, and that's not automatically a gap.
- Margin only improves if a change to one activity doesn't quietly inflate the cost of another (e.g. cutting inbound logistics cost by buying cheaper materials that increase scrap in operations) — always check the chain as a system, not activity by activity in isolation.
- "Keeping up with developments" and "organizing procedures" are easy slide bullets and easy to skip in practice — anchor them to a concrete cadence (e.g. a quarterly review) or they won't happen.
