# Basic Start-Up Infrastructure Checklist

Before a new project can turn assets into output, it needs the physical and utility infrastructure to operate at all — a place to work, the equipment and furniture to work with, reliable power, and a way to communicate. This is a distinct, practical checklist from asset classification: it's not about *what kind* of asset something is, it's about *making sure nothing essential got left off the launch budget*.

## When to use

- Scoping the launch budget for any physical (non-purely-digital) small business, before the founder has committed to a lease or bought equipment.
- Reviewing a business plan that jumps straight from "the idea" to "the marketing plan" without ever pricing out where the work actually happens.
- Sanity-checking a founder who has priced production equipment (see [asset-types.md](asset-types.md)) but hasn't separately priced the infrastructure that equipment needs to sit inside and run on.

## How it works

Five categories cover the basic start-up infrastructure need, in addition to the production assets and machinery themselves:

1. **Location** — chosen based on ease of access for customers *and* for workers, availability of public transportation and parking, and sized to the number of employees and expected lead/production volume. The choice must also comply with safety and health requirements and regulations — this isn't optional polish, it's a launch blocker if skipped.
2. **Office equipment** — the type and quantity of equipment the work itself requires (this is deliberately separate from production machinery — it's the equipment that runs the business *around* production: computers, printers, tools of the trade).
3. **Office furniture** — largely desks, chairs, storage, and the furnishings needed for staff to actually occupy the location.
4. **Power supply requirements** — sized to the total connected electrical load. The deck's specific recommendation: install a UPS (uninterruptible power supply) to protect sensitive devices from sudden outages, and size that UPS to the sum of every electrical appliance it needs to protect, not just a guess.
5. **Communication equipment** — internet connectivity and telephone lines, with the required connection quality/speed and number of lines driven by how the business actually operates (a business running customer support over the phone needs a different spec than one that's purely digital-order-intake).

## Example

A small agricultural mushroom-cultivation project needs to prepare a shed to grow one and a half tons of mushroom over a 60-day cycle, using a modern irrigation, cooling, and sterilization system. Before pricing the growing equipment itself (barrels, burners, irrigation unit, fans, cooling unit — see [asset-types.md](asset-types.md)), the founder still has to separately account for: the location itself (a shed with safe, accessible siting), power sufficient to run the cooling and irrigation systems continuously (with a UPS sized to that load, since a power interruption mid-cycle can ruin the crop), and whatever minimal communication line is needed to coordinate deliveries to the factories that buy the harvest.

## Applying it for a client

Run this as a literal checklist against the client's launch budget, item by item, specifically looking for what's *missing* rather than what's there — founders reliably price the "exciting" line items (the product, the marketing) and forget the boring ones (a UPS, enough phone lines, whether the location is even zoned/accessible for the expected foot traffic or delivery trucks). For any client running production that's sensitive to power interruption (refrigeration, sterilization, anything perishable), push the UPS conversation specifically — undersizing it is one of the most common and most expensive early-stage mistakes, because the failure mode isn't "the lights flicker," it's "the whole batch is ruined."

## Watch-outs

- Don't let "we'll figure out the office later" survive past the budgeting stage — location constraints (accessibility, zoning, safety compliance) can invalidate a business model that looked fine on paper.
- A UPS sized off a guess rather than the actual sum of connected load is a false sense of security — get the real wattage total.
- Communication needs scale with customer-facing model, not with company size — a two-person business doing live customer support over the phone may need more lines than a ten-person business that only takes orders online.
- This checklist assumes a physical operating location; a fully remote/digital business still needs equivalents (a reliable connection, appropriate devices, a place someone actually works), just resized.
