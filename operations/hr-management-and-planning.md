# HR Management and Planning

HR management is the organizational process of comprehensively managing the people inside a business — hiring them, setting their wages, hours, benefits and incentives, and then continuing to manage their performance, guide them through change, and develop them so the organization can actually reach its goals. HR *planning* is the forward-looking half of that: deciding, ahead of need, how many people, with what skills, the organization will require and how it will get them there.

## When to use

- Any point a founder is about to hire — planning should precede the hire, not follow it, or the org ends up staffed reactively around whoever was available rather than around what the work actually needs.
- When a small team is underperforming and the cause isn't a single individual's competence, but organizational climate, unclear coordination, or unaddressed wage/stability concerns.
- When mapping the value chain's human-resources support activity (see [value-chain.md](value-chain.md)) into an actual roster and payroll for a specific client.
- When deciding whom to hire for an early-stage team, where the traits that predict success look nothing like the traits that predict success in a large, structured organization.

## How it works

### What HR management actually covers, day to day

Four areas of ongoing attention define the practice:

1. **Organizational climate and motivation** — providing a work environment that keeps employees motivated, which shows up directly in job satisfaction and, from there, in productivity. This is done in service of the project's strategic objectives, by deliberately drawing on the competencies the team already has rather than assuming new ones are needed.
2. **Training, stability, and compensation** — investing in employee training, working to keep people in their roles (job stability), and paying close attention to wages and salaries — all in service of sustaining and increasing productivity.
3. **Problem discovery and resolution** — surfacing problems and difficulties affecting individuals before they degrade the project's effectiveness, and helping managers actually resolve them through advice and guidance, rather than leaving them to fester.
4. **Cross-unit coordination** — paying attention to coordination between the project's different units, and to the activities that touch every individual across the org, not just within their own silo.

### The five-step HR planning process

1. **Determine the goal** — set both short-term and long-term goals for HR planning specifically, not just for the business generally.
2. **Study and analyze the environment** — an in-depth read of everything surrounding the project that directly affects its strategy. This has two halves: the *internal* environment (what the org can actually do with what it has), and the *external* environment (what talent is available to attract, and what threats — competitors poaching, market shifts — exist).
3. **Forecast human needs** — translate the quality and volume of work required into the actual number and type of people needed to do it. This is the step that turns "we're busy" into "we need two more people, with these specific skills."
4. **Develop the HR plan** — build out employee skills and abilities, and align professional development with the direction the organization is actually moving in. Three preconditions have to be in place here: clarity on the org's intended path, senior management's genuine acceptance and support of the plan, and confirmed availability of the material, financial, and human resources the plan assumes.
5. **Implement and follow up (oversight)** — actively oversee execution, to confirm the plan is being carried out as designed and that the resources committed are actually being used in the project's interest, in service of its stated objectives.

### Characteristics of employees who succeed in small projects

Small, early-stage teams need a different profile than a large, mature organization does — structure, defined roles, and deep specialization are luxuries a two-to-six-person team usually can't afford yet. The traits the program calls out:

- **Problem-solving skills** — the ability to work through issues without an established playbook or a specialist to hand it to.
- **Dependability** — doing what was said would be done, reliably, without close supervision.
- **Flexibility** — willingness to move between tasks and roles as the small team's needs shift day to day.
- **Perseverance** — sticking with a difficult problem or a hard early stretch rather than folding at the first obstacle.
- **Team support** — actively helping teammates, not just protecting one's own lane.
- **Initiative** — taking action and proposing improvements without being told to.
- **Desire to learn** — willingness to pick up new skills, since small teams can't afford narrow specialists who only do one thing.

## Example

A small startup builds a six-role team to run its early operation (deck's own figures, monthly, currency implied EGP):

| Role | Responsibility | Monthly cost |
|---|---|---|
| Admin & accounts | Manages administration and accounts | 400 |
| Technical support | Solves technical issues, provides consultation | 300 |
| 3D designer | Produces required 3D designs | 300 |
| Operations officer | Runs the machines, executes and delivers products | 400 |
| Digital promotion | Handles website and social-media promotion | 250 |
| Marketing | Gets the idea in front of as many customers as possible | 250 |
| **Total monthly payroll** | | **1,900** |

Notice what this roster is *not*: it isn't six deep specialists each doing one narrow thing full-time. It's six roles sized to keep a small operation running, and in practice several of these roles are often the same one or two people wearing multiple hats — which is exactly why the "characteristics of employees in small projects" (flexibility, initiative, desire to learn) matter more here than formal specialization would.

## Applying it for a client

Before helping a client write a job description, run the five-step planning sequence with them, even in compressed form — most small-team hiring goes wrong at step 3 (forecasting needs), where founders hire for a title they've heard of rather than for the actual volume and quality of work in front of them. When screening early hires, weight the small-team traits (flexibility, initiative, dependability, desire to learn) at least as heavily as the technical skill on the job description — a technically strong hire who can't operate without defined process will struggle in a team that doesn't have defined process yet to give them. Use the roster-and-payroll example as a template: for any early client, build the same simple table (role, responsibility, monthly cost) and check it against their actual cash runway before they commit to headcount — this is the fastest way to catch a hiring plan the business can't actually afford.

## Watch-outs

- Don't import a large-org HR playbook (rigid job ladders, narrow role specialization, formal performance-review cycles) wholesale into a small team — it will fight the flexibility the team actually needs to survive.
- "Forecasting human needs" is often skipped in practice because it feels like paperwork for a two-person team — but skipping it is exactly how founders end up hiring the wrong role, or the right role at the wrong time.
- Job stability and wage attention aren't a "nice to have" line item — for a small team, losing one person is losing a much larger fraction of total capability than in a large org, so retention deserves real planning weight even pre-revenue.
- The five-step process assumes senior management buy-in exists before implementation (step 4) — for a founder-led small team this usually just means the founder has actually committed the budget, not merely liked the plan in the abstract.
