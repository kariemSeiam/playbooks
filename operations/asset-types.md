# Asset Types

An asset is anything a company owns that carries real material value and belongs on its books — its annual budget or balance sheet. The reason a founder or consultant needs more than one label for "asset" is that different asset types behave completely differently when the business needs cash, needs collateral, or needs to know what it's actually worth — and a plan that treats all assets as interchangeable will misjudge the business's real financial flexibility.

## When to use

- Building a startup or expansion budget: every line item the founder lists (a laptop, a lease, a patent, a cash reserve) is a different asset type with different cash implications, and grouping them correctly changes what the budget can actually tell you.
- Assessing a client's real liquidity: "we have assets" is not the same claim as "we have cash" — knowing which of a client's assets convert to cash quickly (and which don't) is the difference between a business that can weather a bad month and one that can't.
- Preparing a client to raise debt or investment: lenders and investors care specifically about which assets can serve as collateral (mortgaged) versus which are locked away by legal restriction (frozen) versus which are just illiquid by nature (fixed).
- Separating personal and business finances for a founder who is used to thinking of "my money" and "the company's money" as one pool.

## How it works

### What makes something a financial asset

A financial asset is the stock of a company's bank balances, bonds, and deposits, together with the tangible physical assets it owns and controls — land, buildings, equipment. Three characteristics define whether something qualifies as an asset at all:

- **Ownership** — the owner has the right to sell it, convert it to cash, or mortgage it. If you can't do any of these with it, it isn't functioning as an asset yet.
- **Exchange value** — it carries a recognized economic value that can be traded or bid on in a market.
- **Resource nature** — it is something that can be invested to generate future economic benefit for the company, not just something sitting idle.

### Classification: three axes, six types

The same physical thing can be sliced along three independent axes. A client's laptop, for instance, is simultaneously a current/liquid asset, a tangible asset, and (usually) an operating asset — the axes aren't competing categories, they're three different questions asked of the same asset.

**1. By how easily it converts into money**

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Liquid / current assets** | Convert to cash quickly and are typically consumed or turned over within a short period. | Cash on hand, short-term deposits, office supplies, furniture inventory, laptops. |
| **Fixed assets** | Cannot be easily converted to cash or its equivalent; defined by their long-term nature. | Land, buildings, heavy machinery, patents, trademarks. |

**2. By physical presence**

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Tangible assets** | Have a real physical presence and occupy space. | Land, buildings, cash notes, office supplies, warehouse contents, machinery, technical equipment. |
| **Intangible assets** | No physical, material presence — their value is legal or informational, not physical. | Patents, property rights, trademarks, copyrights. |

**3. By use**

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating assets** | Used to run daily operations and generate additional revenue; must be kept available for the business to function. | Machinery, technical equipment, copyrights actively in use, working cash flow. |
| **Non-operating assets** | Not required to run daily operations, but still retain strategic value. | Investments, land held but not used, fixed-term deposits. |

### Other forms worth naming specifically

Beyond the three-axis classification, a few asset conditions come up often enough in a small business's life that they deserve their own vocabulary:

- **Frozen (restricted) assets** — assets the owner cannot currently liquidate, sell, or otherwise access, typically because of a legal restriction: a government order, a court order, or an unresolved debt. The defining feature is that ownership hasn't necessarily changed — access has been cut off. This matters operationally because a client can look "asset-rich" on paper while a frozen asset gives them zero real liquidity.
- **Mortgaged assets** — assets pledged as security for a loan without transferring ownership or possession. The lender has a claim on the asset if the loan defaults, but the business keeps using it in the meantime — this is how a client can raise financing against equipment or property they still need to operate.
- **Personal assets** — assets an individual (not the company) owns and can convert to cash: a home, a car, jewelry, a savings or retirement account. Naming this separately matters most for early-stage founders who are informally funding the business out of personal assets — the moment that happens, the two should be tracked separately, not blended.

## Example

A small mushroom-cultivation startup needs the following equipment before it can begin production (deck's own figures, currency implied EGP):

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Surface barrels (2x, 100L capacity) | 100 |
| Burners (2x) | 300 |
| Irrigation unit (1x) | 760 |
| Pumps (2x) | 500 |
| Extractor fans (4x) | 945 |
| Refrigeration/cooling unit | 1,400 |
| Scale | 150 |
| Thermometer | 120 |
| Hygrometer + irrigation network | 760 |
| **Total equipment investment** | **5,035** |

Every one of these is a **tangible, operating, fixed** asset — physically present, actively used in daily production, and not something the founder can liquidate quickly without halting the operation. That's exactly the profile a founder needs to plan cash for *before* launch, because none of it converts back to cash if the founder underestimates working-capital needs later.

## Applying it for a client

Walk the client's full asset list once, but tag every item along all three axes rather than picking one classification and stopping — the interesting findings usually live in the overlaps. A client who believes they have "plenty of assets" but whose assets are mostly fixed, non-operating, or frozen has a liquidity problem they haven't recognized yet; surfacing that early prevents a cash crunch from looking like a surprise. When a client is preparing to raise debt, walk them specifically through which assets could be offered as collateral (mortgaged) without disrupting operations, versus which ones they actually need to keep unencumbered because they're operating assets. And whenever a founder is self-funding, insist on a clean split between their personal assets and the company's — a personal car or home used informally as collateral for the business needs to be named and tracked as exactly that, not folded silently into "the business's assets."

## Watch-outs

- The source deck's own "frozen assets" and "fake assets" slide is garbled — one box's definition is a copy-paste from an unrelated section, and the other actually describes what "frozen" means. This note renders the single intended concept (illiquid/restricted assets) under "frozen (restricted) assets" above and drops the duplicate "fake assets" label rather than propagate the error.
- The three axes genuinely overlap — a patent shows up as "fixed" under the convertibility axis and as "intangible" under the physical-presence axis. That's expected, not a contradiction; don't force a single master category per asset.
- "Fixed" (hard to convert to cash) and "non-operating" (not needed for daily operations) sound similar but aren't the same test — a piece of idle land can be both, but a piece of essential heavy machinery is fixed *and* operating at the same time.
- A frozen or mortgaged asset still appears on the books at full value — don't let a client's balance sheet visually overstate their actual financial flexibility; always ask what fraction of listed assets are genuinely liquid *today*.
- Small operations tend to under-invest in classifying their own assets at all, because the list feels short — but the discipline scales down fine, and skipping it is exactly what leads a founder to be blindsided when they need cash and discover most of what they "own" can't be turned into it quickly.
