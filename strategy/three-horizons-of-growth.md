# Three Horizons of Growth

The Three Horizons model, developed by McKinsey consultants Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley, and David White in their 1999 book *The Alchemy of Growth*, answers a problem that pure portfolio tools like [bcg-growth-share-matrix.md](bcg-growth-share-matrix.md) don't fully address: how does a company keep growing *over time*, not just this year? Their argument is that sustained growth requires managing three horizons of opportunity simultaneously — the core business generating cash today, the emerging businesses that will be tomorrow's core, and the options being seeded for the business after that — because a company that only manages Horizon 1 eventually runs out of growth, while a company that neglects Horizon 1 to chase Horizon 3 runs out of cash first. A consultant needs it because clients under short-term pressure (quarterly earnings, annual budget cycles) systematically under-invest in Horizons 2 and 3, and the model is the clearest way to make that trade-off visible and correctable.

## When to use

- A client's growth has plateaued and the org has been managing entirely off the current core business's metrics, with no visible pipeline of what replaces that core as it matures or declines.
- Building a long-term innovation or growth-investment portfolio and needing to justify why *not every dollar* should chase this year's highest-return opportunity.
- A board or CFO is pushing to defund "unproven" initiatives (typically Horizon 2/3 bets) in favor of near-term core-business ROI, and the client needs a framework to make the long-term cost of that trade-off explicit.
- Complementing [bcg-growth-share-matrix.md](bcg-growth-share-matrix.md) or [ge-mckinsey-nine-box.md](ge-mckinsey-nine-box.md) portfolio work with an explicit time dimension — those tools show *where* value sits today; Three Horizons shows *when* today's bets will (or won't) mature.
- Structuring an innovation function or venture arm and needing distinct governance, metrics, and funding models for each horizon rather than one-size-fits-all rules.

## How it works

### The three horizons

- **Horizon 1 (H1) — Core** — the current business generating most of today's revenue and profit. Well-understood, well-defined customers and competitors, incremental improvement opportunities (efficiency, share gains, line extensions). This is the primary source of cash funding the other two horizons, and the primary focus of near-term financial reporting and operational management. Time horizon: typically the current year to next 1-2 years.

- **Horizon 2 (H2) — Emerging** — businesses and initiatives with proven early traction but not yet at scale: a new product line finding product-market fit, a geographic expansion ramping up, an adjacent market entry gaining share. These require sustained investment and a different management approach than H1 (growth metrics and market-building milestones rather than efficiency and margin optimization), because judging them by H1's mature-business metrics kills them prematurely. Time horizon: typically 2-3 years out from meaningful scale.

- **Horizon 3 (H3) — Future options** — early-stage bets, R&D projects, pilots, and options on future business models that may or may not pan out — most won't, and that's expected and acceptable, because H3 is managed as a portfolio of options, not individual bets each expected to succeed. The purpose is to seed the businesses that will become H2 and eventually H1 several years out, ensuring the company isn't caught flat-footed when the current core matures or gets disrupted. Time horizon: typically 3+ years out, often exploratory with no committed revenue target at all.

### The overlapping S-curve logic

The model is best visualized as three overlapping growth S-curves plotted over time: H1's curve is flattening (approaching maturity), H2's curve is in its steep growth phase, and H3's curve is just beginning to lift off the baseline. The strategic point is that a healthy company always has all three curves running *simultaneously*, staggered so that as H1 flattens, H2 is maturing into the next core, and H3 is being seeded to eventually become the H2 after that — a continuous relay rather than a one-time pivot. A company that manages only H1 will see growth stall the moment the core matures, with nothing in the pipeline to replace it; a company that starves H1 to overfund H3 speculative bets risks a cash crisis before those bets mature, if they ever do.

### Different rules for different horizons

The model's operational insight is that each horizon needs a different management approach, and applying H1's rules to H2 or H3 (or vice versa) is the most common implementation failure:

| | Horizon 1 | Horizon 2 | Horizon 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary metric** | Profit, margin, efficiency | Revenue growth rate, market traction, unit economics trending toward viability | Learning velocity, number of validated/invalidated hypotheses, option value |
| **Funding approach** | Standard operating budget | Dedicated growth investment, protected from short-term P&L pressure | Small, staged bets across a portfolio; expect most to fail |
| **Governance/talent** | Experienced operators, process discipline | Builder/general-manager profile, comfortable with ambiguity but focused on scaling | Explorer/entrepreneur profile, comfortable killing an idea quickly if it's not working |
| **Time horizon for payback** | This year | 1-3 years | 3+ years, often no committed payback date |

## Example

**A traditional print media company mapped across the three horizons:**

- **Horizon 1:** The print newspaper and its associated print advertising revenue — declining but still the largest cash generator, managed for efficiency (cost discipline, retained subscriber base) rather than growth.
- **Horizon 2:** The company's digital subscription product — already past initial product-market fit, growing at double-digit rates year over year, requiring continued investment in product, content, and marketing to reach the scale where it can eventually replace print as the core revenue engine. Managed with growth-stage metrics (subscriber growth, churn, engagement) rather than the print business's efficiency metrics.
- **Horizon 3:** Early-stage experiments — a data/analytics product for advertisers built on the company's audience insights, a podcast network, an events business — each funded as a small, time-boxed pilot with explicit kill criteria, none expected to be individually large yet, collectively serving as the seed portfolio for whichever one (if any) becomes the next Horizon 2 in 3-5 years.

The consulting diagnosis for a media company that only manages Horizon 1 well: print revenue keeps declining, digital subscriptions are underfunded relative to their actual growth potential because leadership keeps pulling budget back to defend print margins, and there's no real Horizon 3 experimentation happening at all — meaning that even once digital subscription growth eventually plateaus, there's nothing seeded to replace it. The recommended intervention is to protect Horizon 2's investment budget from being raided to prop up Horizon 1's margins, and to formally fund a small portfolio of Horizon 3 pilots with dedicated (if modest) budget and explicit governance, rather than treating all innovation spend as a rounding error to be cut whenever the core business has a bad quarter.

## Applying it for a client

Start by mapping the client's *current* portfolio of activities and investments onto the three horizons honestly — many clients discover, once forced to categorize, that nearly all their investment sits in H1 (defending or slightly improving the core) with a token H2 initiative and effectively no H3 activity at all; that imbalance, made visible, is usually the single most persuasive finding in the engagement. Insist each horizon get its own metrics and its own governance conversation rather than being reviewed in the same meeting with the same criteria as the core business — an H2 initiative judged on H1's margin expectations will be defunded before it has time to reach scale, and an H3 pilot expected to show H1-style ROI will never get funded at all. When a client's board pressures cutting "unproven" initiatives during a downturn, use the model to frame the trade-off explicitly: cutting H2/H3 protects this year's numbers but predictably produces a growth gap in 2-3 years when H1 has matured further and nothing has been built to replace it — make that future gap a forecastable, discussable line item now rather than a surprise later.

## Watch-outs

- The horizons are not a maturity ladder every initiative must climb in order — many H3 bets are correctly killed and never become H2; the model expects and requires this attrition, so don't treat an H3 pilot's "failure" as evidence the framework or the team failed.
- Resist the common mistake of relabeling routine product-line extensions as "innovation" (H2 or H3) to make the innovation pipeline look healthier than it is — an incremental improvement to the core, however useful, is H1 work and should be funded and measured as such.
- H2 is the horizon most likely to be starved in practice, precisely because it's neither the safe, familiar core (H1) nor the cheap, low-commitment experiment (H3) — it requires real sustained investment during the exact stage when its returns are least proven, which is uncomfortable for budget cycles built around annual ROI justification.
- The model describes a portfolio logic over time — it doesn't replace [ansoff-matrix.md](ansoff-matrix.md) for classifying what *kind* of growth bet each horizon investment actually is (penetration, new product, new market, diversification); use both together, since an H3 bet is very often also a Diversification-quadrant bet and should be evaluated with both lenses.
- Time-horizon boundaries (1-2 years, 2-3 years, 3+ years) are illustrative, not fixed — industry clock speed matters enormously (a biotech H3 bet may take a decade; a consumer app's H2-to-H1 transition may take 18 months); recalibrate the horizons' actual timeframes to the client's industry rather than importing generic figures.
