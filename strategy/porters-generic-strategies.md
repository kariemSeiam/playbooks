# Porter's Generic Strategies

Generic Strategies is Michael Porter's answer to the question "given the industry structure, how does *this* firm actually win?" — the firm-level companion to [porters-five-forces.md](porters-five-forces.md), which only tells you whether the industry itself is attractive. Porter's argument, laid out in his 1985 follow-up to *Competitive Strategy*, is that there are only two fundamental sources of competitive advantage — lower cost or differentiation — and combining them with competitive scope (broad market or narrow segment) yields exactly four viable positions. A consultant needs this because the single most common cause of client underperformance Porter identified isn't picking the wrong strategy — it's picking no strategy, or trying to run two incompatible ones at once ("stuck in the middle").

## When to use

- A client has clear industry-attractiveness data (from Five Forces) but hasn't translated it into a firm-level competitive position.
- Margins are mediocre despite decent revenue — often a sign the client is stuck in the middle, competing on both price and features without committing to either.
- A client is choosing between competing on cost versus building a premium/differentiated brand and needs the trade-offs made explicit before committing budget.
- Evaluating whether a niche player should try to go broad-market, or whether a broad player is vulnerable to a focused entrant undercutting them in one segment.
- Post-merger strategy work, when two firms with different generic strategies are being combined and the combined entity needs one coherent position, not two.

## How it works

Porter starts from the **value chain** — the set of discrete activities a firm performs to design, produce, market, deliver, and support its product (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service, plus support activities: infrastructure, HR, technology development, procurement). Competitive advantage comes from configuring and linking these activities differently — and cheaper, or differently — and better, than rivals. Two dimensions define the strategic choices available:

**Dimension 1 — Source of advantage:** Lower cost or differentiation.
**Dimension 2 — Competitive scope:** Broad (industry-wide) or narrow (a particular segment).

Crossing them gives four generic strategies:

| | Broad target | Narrow target |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower cost** | Cost Leadership | Cost Focus |
| **Differentiation** | Differentiation | Differentiation Focus |

**1. Cost Leadership** — become the industry's lowest-cost producer while maintaining roughly-parity quality, then either underprice competitors to gain share or match prices and earn superior margins. Achieved through aggressive pursuit of scale economies, tight cost control across every activity, minimizing spend in R&D/service/advertising where it doesn't affect the core offer, and often a strong emphasis on the experience/learning curve. Examples of the mechanism: bulk purchasing, standardized product lines, process efficiency, low-cost distribution.

**2. Differentiation** — offer something perceived industry-wide as unique on a dimension buyers value: design, brand image, technology, features, customer service, or dealer network. Differentiation earns a price premium if the premium exceeds the extra cost of being different, and it builds buyer loyalty that acts as a barrier — buyers become less price-sensitive when the alternative isn't a true substitute for what they value.

**3. Cost Focus** — pursue a cost advantage within a narrow segment only, exploiting the fact that broad-scope cost leaders have to serve segment needs they don't optimize for. The focuser's costs may be structurally lower for *that segment's* specific requirements than a generalist's.

**4. Differentiation Focus** — pursue differentiation within a narrow segment, exploiting the buyer needs of that segment that broad differentiators serve imperfectly because they're optimizing for the average buyer across all segments.

### Why "stuck in the middle" fails

Porter's sharpest claim: a firm that fails to commit to one of the four positions is "stuck in the middle" — it lacks the market share, capital investment, and resolve to play the cost-minimization game, and lacks the differentiation needed to avoid a low-cost, high-volume competitor undercutting it, and lacks the focus needed to create advantage in a narrower sphere. It ends up with below-average profitability, competing on both dimensions poorly rather than one dimension excellently — an unstable position, because *any* competitor that clearly commits to one generic strategy is better positioned to compete for any segment.

### Requirements and risks per strategy

| Strategy | Common prerequisites | Common risks |
|---|---|---|
| Cost Leadership | Sustained capital investment, process engineering skill, tight supervision, cost-based control systems | Technology change nullifying past investment/learning; imitators; loss of ability to spot needed product/marketing changes because cost focus dominates attention |
| Differentiation | Strong marketing, product engineering, creative flair, cross-functional coordination, reputation for quality | The cost differential vs. low-cost competitors grows too large for brand loyalty to hold; buyers' need for the differentiating factor falls; imitation narrows perceived differentiation |
| Cost/Differentiation Focus | Above, but combined and targeted at a particular segment | Broad-scope competitors find ways to serve the segment as well; the segment's needs converge back toward the broader market; other focusers sub-segment further |

## Example

**Airlines — same industry, all four positions occupied simultaneously:**

- **Cost Leadership (broad):** Ryanair / Southwest — standardized single aircraft type, no-frills service, secondary airports, high aircraft utilization, ancillary-fee revenue model. Broad geographic scope, relentless cost focus.
- **Differentiation (broad):** Emirates / Singapore Airlines — premium cabin experience, service reputation, extensive route network as part of the value proposition, willing to spend on the differentiator (service, brand) that broad-scope buyers value across the entire route map.
- **Cost Focus (narrow):** A regional low-cost carrier serving one dense short-haul corridor others find unprofitable to serve cheaply — optimizing cost structure for that corridor's specific demand pattern rather than a national network.
- **Differentiation Focus (narrow):** A private-jet charter or ultra-long-haul first-class-only carrier serving one narrow, high-value segment (business travelers on a specific route) with a service level broad differentiators wouldn't build for their whole network.

Note what's structurally *absent*: an airline trying to be "somewhat cheap and somewhat premium, everywhere" — that's the stuck-in-the-middle position, and it's rare to find a surviving example, because a Ryanair-type competitor undercuts it on cost while an Emirates-type competitor outclasses it on service.

## Applying it for a client

Start by naming the client's *current* position honestly, even if it's the uncomfortable answer — many clients believe they're "differentiated" when their actual cost structure and pricing behavior say cost-focused, or vice versa. Use the value chain (inbound logistics through service, plus support activities) to locate exactly where the client's cost or differentiation actually comes from — a vague answer like "our people" doesn't survive contact with the value chain; a specific answer like "our procurement relationships give us input costs 12% below the segment average" does. If the diagnosis is stuck-in-the-middle — decent quality, decent price, no clear reason to choose this firm over either a cheaper or a more premium alternative — the recommendation is rarely "do both better"; it's usually "pick one and cut the investment that doesn't serve it." For a smaller or resource-constrained client, default the conversation toward the two Focus strategies before broad-scope ones — a narrow segment is where a client with limited capital can credibly claim cost or differentiation leadership against competitors who can't be bothered to specialize.

## Watch-outs

- The four strategies are not "pick your favorite" — they depend on the industry structure identified in [porters-five-forces.md](porters-five-forces.md); a strategy that ignores buyer power or entry barriers can look coherent on paper and fail in the market.
- Differentiation does not mean "add more features" — it means adding a feature buyers actually value enough to pay a premium for; over-differentiation past what buyers value is wasted cost with no pricing power to show for it.
- Cost leadership is a trap for firms that mistake "cutting costs" for "having the lowest cost structure" — real cost leadership requires structural advantages (scale, proprietary process, location), not just a round of expense cuts that competitors can copy in a quarter.
- A focused strategy's segment can erode if the broader-scope competitors decide the segment is worth pursuing directly — a focuser's real defense is that the segment isn't attractive enough, structurally, for the broad player to chase (see [porters-five-forces.md](porters-five-forces.md) on entry barriers within a sub-segment).
- Don't confuse "stuck in the middle" with a deliberate hybrid strategy done well (e.g., a firm using automation to be both lower-cost *and* higher-quality than legacy competitors) — those cases are rare and usually rest on a genuine technological or operational discontinuity, not wishful positioning.
