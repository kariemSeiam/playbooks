# Scenario Planning

Scenario planning is a structured method for exploring several plausible, internally consistent futures rather than betting a strategy on a single forecast. It descends from RAND's Cold War military scenario work (Herman Kahn), was turned into a business discipline by Pierre Wack's planning team at Royal Dutch Shell in the early 1970s, and was codified into a repeatable methodology by Peter Schwartz — who worked under Wack at Shell before founding Global Business Network — in "The Art of the Long View" (1991). A consultant needs it because forecasting answers "what will happen" (and is usually wrong past a short horizon), while scenario planning answers "what could happen, and is our strategy robust across all of it" — the only honest question to ask under genuine uncertainty.

## When to use
- Long planning horizons (5–20 years) in domains with high structural uncertainty — energy, geopolitics, technology disruption, regulation
- Board or executive strategy offsites need a shared vocabulary for "what could happen" before committing capital
- A client is over-anchored on a single base-case forecast and the plan needs pressure-testing against alternative futures
- Entering a new market or regulatory environment where several futures are genuinely plausible, not just one extrapolated trend
- Pre-major-investment or post-crisis decisions where downside protection matters as much as upside capture

## How it works

**The 9-step process:**
1. **Define the focal issue** — the specific decision or question driving the exercise, with an explicit time horizon
2. **Identify key forces** in the local environment (customers, competitors, suppliers, regulators)
3. **Identify driving forces** at the macro level (social, technological, economic, environmental, political — see [pestel-analysis.md](../marketing/pestel-analysis.md))
4. **Rank every force** on two criteria: importance to the focal decision, and degree of uncertainty
5. **Select the two most important and most uncertain forces** as the axes of a 2×2 matrix — these become the "critical uncertainties"
6. **Write scenario logics** — flesh out all four quadrants as plausible, internally consistent narratives, not just labels
7. **Derive implications** — for each scenario, what happens to the business, the market, the decision at hand
8. **Identify leading indicators** — concrete, observable signposts that would tell you which scenario is actually emerging
9. **Test the current strategy against all four scenarios** — a genuinely robust strategy survives in all four, or the exercise surfaces where contingent, option-based moves are needed for specific ones

**First-generation vs. second-generation scenarios.** Wack drew a sharp distinction that most practitioners since have flattened: first-generation scenarios are just alternative descriptions of the future (useful, but easy to file and ignore). Second-generation scenarios are built specifically around how the decision-maker currently perceives reality, designed to challenge and change that perception — Wack's own phrase was that good scenarios must cause managers to "re-perceive reality." A scenario set that doesn't unsettle at least one comfortable assumption in the room hasn't done its job.

**Critical uncertainty vs. predetermined element.** Forces that are important but *not* uncertain (demographic trends, physical constraints) are predetermined elements — hold them constant across every scenario. Only forces that are both important *and* genuinely uncertain belong on the 2×2 axes. Conflating the two is the most common design error (see Watch-outs).

## Example

In 1971–72, Pierre Wack's planning team at Shell built scenarios that included a future where oil-producing nations gained real pricing power and shocked the market — grounded in observable signals already visible at the time (OPEC's formation in 1960, rising Middle East tensions). When the 1973 Yom Kippur War triggered the OPEC oil embargo and prices quadrupled almost overnight, Shell's management — having already rehearsed decisions for that world — reacted faster than every other major oil company: adjusting refining investment, upstream negotiating posture, and capital allocation ahead of rivals still reacting to the shock as a surprise. Shell rose from among the weakest of the "Seven Sisters" oil majors to among the strongest through the 1980s, and the case became the founding proof that scenario planning changes outcomes, not just conversations.

## Applying it for a client

Run it as a live 1–2 day workshop with the actual leadership team, not a document produced solo and presented back. Cap the output at three to four scenarios — Schwartz's own practice caps at four because more become unusable for real decision-making. Give each scenario a memorable name (Schwartz's GBN convention — "Boomtown," not "Scenario A") so it survives in institutional memory past the workshop. Anchor the exercise to an actual pending decision (the focal issue in step 1), not a generic "what's the future of our industry" — scenarios built without a specific decision attached rarely get used. Revisit and refresh the set periodically as driving forces shift; Shell itself refreshes its scenarios every few years rather than treating any set as permanent.

## Watch-outs
- Scenario planning is not forecasting — if a client asks "which one will actually happen," expectations were mis-set; the deliverable is preparedness across futures, not a prediction of the winning one
- More than four scenarios dilutes decision-usefulness; discipline to the two most critical, most uncertain axes
- Confusing a predetermined trend (belongs as a constant across every scenario) with a critical uncertainty (belongs on an axis) is the single most common design mistake
- "Best case / base case / worst case" along one axis is sensitivity analysis, not scenario planning — real scenarios must be structurally different worlds, not points on a spectrum
- Building polished scenarios and never reconnecting them to an actual decision turns the exercise into strategic theater rather than the re-perception Wack intended
