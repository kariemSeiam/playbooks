# Porter's Five Forces

Five Forces is a framework for diagnosing why an industry is or isn't profitable — not why one company beats another. Michael Porter published it in a 1979 HBR article arguing that competitive strategy people were too focused on rivals and ignoring the other four sources of competitive pressure that actually determine the ceiling on an industry's long-run profitability. A consultant needs it because "the market is growing" and "the market is profitable" are different claims, and clients routinely confuse them — a fast-growing industry with weak forces (easy entry, powerful suppliers) can still be a terrible place to make money.

## When to use

- A client is evaluating whether to enter a new industry or market segment and wants more than a growth-rate number to justify the bet.
- A client's margins have been eroding for years and no one internally can say whether it's execution (fixable) or industry structure (not fixable without changing the game).
- Due diligence on an acquisition target — the target's historical profitability only holds if the industry's structural forces stay the same.
- Building the industry-context section of any strategy engagement, before moving to firm-level tools like [porters-generic-strategies.md](porters-generic-strategies.md) or [vrio-framework.md](vrio-framework.md).
- A client believes they have no competitors ("we're the only ones doing X") — the Five Forces catches substitutes and potential entrants that a narrow competitor scan misses.

## How it works

Porter's core claim: the state of competition in an industry depends on five forces, and the collective strength of those forces determines the industry's ultimate profit potential — measured as long-run return on invested capital relative to cost of capital. The forces jointly determine how much of the value created in the industry is captured by incumbent firms versus bled away to suppliers, customers, substitutes, or new entrants.

### The five forces

**1. Threat of new entrants**
New entrants bring capacity and a desire for market share, which pressures prices, costs, and investment. The threat depends on barriers to entry and the reaction incumbents are expected to mount. Key barriers:
- **Economies of scale** — forces entrants to come in at large scale (risking retaliation) or small scale (accepting a cost disadvantage).
- **Capital requirements** — large up-front investment (R&D, advertising, inventory) deters entry, especially if it's a sunk, unrecoverable cost.
- **Switching costs** — one-time costs a buyer faces to switch supplier (retraining, new equipment, re-engineering).
- **Access to distribution channels** — the more a new entrant has to buy, squeeze, or create shelf space, the higher the barrier.
- **Cost disadvantages independent of scale** — proprietary technology, favorable access to raw materials, favorable locations, learning-curve advantages, government subsidies.
- **Government policy** — licensing requirements, limits on raw material access, environmental/safety regulation.
- **Expected retaliation** — a history of aggressive incumbent response, incumbents with resources to fight, or slow industry growth (any entrant's gain is someone's loss) all raise expected retaliation.

**2. Bargaining power of suppliers**
Suppliers can squeeze industry profitability by raising prices or cutting quality. A supplier group is powerful when:
- It's dominated by few companies and more concentrated than the industry it sells to.
- Its product has no substitutes.
- The industry is not an important customer to the supplier group.
- The supplier's product is an important input to the buyer's business.
- The supplier group's products are differentiated or have switching costs built up.
- The supplier group poses a credible threat of forward integration (becoming a competitor).

**3. Bargaining power of buyers**
Buyers compete with the industry by forcing down prices, demanding higher quality or more service, and playing competitors off against each other. A buyer group is powerful when:
- It's concentrated or purchases large volumes relative to seller sales.
- The products it purchases are standard/undifferentiated (buyers can always find alternate suppliers).
- It faces few switching costs.
- It earns low profits (creates pressure to cut purchasing costs).
- The industry's product is unimportant to the quality of the buyer's own products/services.
- It poses a credible threat of backward integration.
- The buyer has full information (about demand, market prices, even supplier costs).

**4. Threat of substitute products or services**
Substitutes limit an industry's potential returns by placing a ceiling on the prices firms can profitably charge — a substitute doesn't have to be a similar product, just a different way to satisfy the same need (e.g., video conferencing as a substitute for business travel). The threat is greatest when substitutes offer an attractive price-performance trade-off, or come from an industry earning high profits (which will push prices down further as substitute producers add capacity).

**5. Rivalry among existing competitors**
Rivalry occurs because competitors feel the pressure or see an opportunity to improve position — through price competition, advertising battles, product introductions, or service increases. Intense rivalry is associated with:
- Numerous or equally balanced competitors (no clear leader to enforce discipline).
- Slow industry growth (competition becomes a fight for share rather than riding growth).
- High fixed or storage costs (pressure to fill capacity, often via price cuts).
- Lack of differentiation or switching costs (competition reduces to price and service).
- Capacity added in large increments (creates chronic overcapacity/underutilization cycles).
- Diverse competitors (different origins, strategies, cultures — harder to read each other's intentions).
- High strategic stakes (a foreign competitor treating the market as essential to its global position will invest even at a loss).
- High exit barriers (specialized assets, fixed costs of exit, strategic interrelationships, emotional barriers, government/social restrictions) — firms keep competing even when returns are low or negative because leaving is more expensive than staying.

### Reading the result

The weakest link isn't necessarily the answer — Porter's point is structural: the *collective* strength of the five forces sets the industry ceiling on returns, and the most important force(s) differ by industry (suppliers matter enormously in commodity extraction; rivalry and substitutes dominate in consumer electronics). The output of a Five Forces analysis is not a single score but a structural map: which forces are strong, which are weak, and — critically — which of them a strategy could actually shift.

## Example

**Specialty coffee shops, mid-sized city, worked through all five forces:**

- **Threat of new entrants — moderate-to-high.** Capital requirements are modest (a small storefront, espresso equipment), switching costs for customers are near zero, and there's no meaningful economies-of-scale barrier at small scale. Partially offset by access to good locations (limited prime real estate) and brand/loyalty effects that take time to build.
- **Supplier power — low-to-moderate.** Green coffee bean suppliers are numerous and coffee is a global commodity with many origins: a shop can switch roasters or bean sources without much friction. Power rises somewhat for a shop dependent on one specialty/rare-origin supplier.
- **Buyer power — high.** Individual customers buy one drink at a time, have zero switching costs (the next coffee shop is a block away), and the product is largely undifferentiated to a casual buyer — a latte is a latte. This is usually the dominant force keeping margins thin in this industry.
- **Threat of substitutes — moderate.** Home espresso machines, instant/pod coffee (Nespresso), energy drinks, and simply not drinking coffee all cap how much a shop can charge before customers substitute away.
- **Rivalry — high.** Low differentiation, low switching costs, and often slow same-store growth in a saturated urban market push shops into service/ambiance/loyalty-program competition rather than pure price war, but margins stay compressed.

**Conclusion:** structurally, independent coffee shops sit in a low-to-moderate profitability industry — not because any owner is running the business badly, but because buyer power and rivalry are both structurally high and entry barriers are low. The strategic implication is to attack the forces that can actually move: build switching costs (loyalty program, a "regular's" relationship, a signature drink unavailable elsewhere) and differentiate against substitutes (an experience Nespresso can't replicate), rather than trying to out-discount the shop next door.

## Applying it for a client

Run the five forces as a structured workshop with whoever in the client's org actually touches suppliers, customers, and competitors day to day — the analysis is only as good as the ground truth going into each force, and a founder's gut feel about "buyer power" is often wrong relative to what their own sales team knows. Score each force qualitatively (low/moderate/high) with two or three bullet reasons, resist the urge to produce a fake composite number — the value is in the reasoning trail, not a 3.4/5. Once the map is built, spend the real strategy time on one question: which force is both strong *and* movable? A client can't out-negotiate a fragmented, price-competitive buyer base with better sales technique, but they can build switching costs (see [blue-ocean-strategy.md](blue-ocean-strategy.md) for how to reposition away from a saturated competitive space) or lock in a supplier relationship that competitors can't replicate. Pair this with [pestel-analysis.md](../marketing/pestel-analysis.md) for macro context feeding into the entrants/substitutes forces, and hand the output to [porters-generic-strategies.md](porters-generic-strategies.md) to pick a firm-level response to the industry structure just mapped.

## Watch-outs

- Five Forces analyzes industry attractiveness, not company competitiveness — a client can operate in a terrible-forces industry and still outperform through superior execution (see [vrio-framework.md](vrio-framework.md)), and conversely a great-forces industry doesn't guarantee any one firm profits.
- Don't treat "competitors" as only direct rivals selling the identical product — substitutes and buyer power are usually the two forces clients underestimate, precisely because they don't look like traditional competition.
- Forces shift over time (deregulation removes an entry barrier, a new technology creates a substitute overnight) — a Five Forces map is a snapshot, not a permanent verdict; revisit it when the industry's technology, regulation, or buyer behavior changes materially.
- The framework describes structure, not action — it tells a client where the pressure comes from, not what to do about it. Pair it with a firm-level strategy tool before calling the engagement done.
- Avoid double-counting: high rivalry and low differentiation often show up as symptoms of the same underlying condition (commoditization) — don't list the same root cause as evidence under two different forces without noting the overlap.
