# First-Principles Thinking

First-principles thinking breaks a problem down to its most basic, independently verifiable truths and reasons back up from there, instead of reasoning by analogy — copying what others do, deferring to industry convention or "best practice." Aristotle defined a first principle as "the first basis from which a thing is known." Its modern business popularity is usually credited to Elon Musk, who has repeatedly described using it to challenge industry-standard cost assumptions in rockets and batteries, and it sits alongside Charlie Munger's "latticework of mental models" as a core tool for questioning inherited assumptions before optimizing within them. A consultant needs it because most client constraints presented as fixed are actually inherited conventions, not laws of physics or economics.

## When to use
- Industry convention or a competitor's approach is being copied without being tested against the client's actual constraints
- Cost or feasibility assumptions are inherited from historical pricing or supplier relationships rather than the underlying physics or economics of the components involved
- A client is stuck because "that's how it's always been done" and needs explicit permission to question the premise, not just optimize the current approach
- The goal is a breakthrough, non-incremental improvement — analogy-based reasoning tends to cap outcomes at incremental variation on whatever is being copied
- Early in a strategy engagement, to pressure-test whether the client's stated constraints are real (physical, legal, economic) or merely assumed (habit, tradition, internal politics)

## How it works

**Two reasoning modes, deliberately contrasted:**
- **Reasoning by analogy** — "X works this way because that's how similar things have always been done, or how competitors do it." Fast and low-effort, but caps outcomes at incremental variation on the existing solution, and silently imports someone else's assumptions and constraints, which may not even apply to your situation.
- **Reasoning by first principles** — "What do we actually, verifiably know to be true here, independent of how anyone else has approached it?" Slower and more effortful, but not bounded by the existing solution space, which is what makes non-incremental outcomes possible.

**Three-step process** (consistent with Musk's own stated method and the classical approach):
1. **Identify and question every assumption** — list everything currently believed about the problem (cost drivers, constraints, "rules"), and for each ask: is this a fundamental law (physics, math, a verified fact) or an assumption inherited from convention, tradition, or someone else's business model?
2. **Break the problem down to fundamental truths** — strip away everything that isn't independently, empirically verifiable. What raw materials, physical constraints, or unit economics actually exist at their most basic level?
3. **Build the solution back up from those fundamentals** — reconstruct a new approach using only what survived step 2, without reference to how the problem has conventionally been solved.

## Example

The aerospace industry priced an orbital rocket at roughly $65 million, and reasoning by analogy treats that as a given — "this is what a rocket costs." Musk instead asked what a rocket is actually made of: aerospace-grade aluminum alloys, titanium, copper, carbon fiber — and calculated the raw material cost at roughly 2% of the typical sale price. That gap between "raw materials are ~2% of price" and "the market price is 100%" revealed that "$65M" was not a fundamental truth but an assumption baked in by the existing industry's manufacturing process and supply chain. SpaceX then rebuilt the approach from the materials up — vertical integration, reusability — rather than trying to incrementally shave costs off the existing method.

## Applying it for a client

Use it specifically when a client presents a constraint as fixed — "we can't do X, it costs too much, it takes too long, that's not how our industry works." Ask them to defend the constraint: is it a law of physics, a regulation, or verified unit economics — or an assumption inherited from a vendor contract, a legacy process, or industry habit? Run it as a structured workshop exercise: list every assumption underlying the current approach on one side, sort each into "fundamental" or "inherited assumption," then rebuild a solution using only the fundamentals column. This is most valuable paired with a cost or feasibility study — the Musk rocket example is really a first-principles cost breakdown, and that is the most transferable version of the technique for a business-consulting context.

## Watch-outs
- First-principles thinking is slow and resource-intensive; using it on every decision is paralysis. Reserve it for decisions where the existing convention is suspected to be wrong, or where the payoff from a breakthrough justifies the effort
- A common failure is stopping the decomposition too early and calling something a "fundamental truth" when it's actually still an assumption one level down — keep asking "how do we know this is true?" until reaching something independently verifiable
- It can produce solutions correct in theory that ignore real (if inconvenient) constraints like organizational politics, regulatory reality, or capital availability — pair the output with a feasibility gut-check, don't ship it raw
- Teams can use "first principles" as a rhetorical flourish to relitigate settled decisions endlessly — the discipline requires actually reaching fundamentals and building back up, not repeatedly asking "but why" without ever arriving at an answer
- It works best on cost, technical, and process problems with knowable fundamentals; it's a weaker fit for problems that are fundamentally about human behavior, politics, or social systems, where [systems-thinking.md](systems-thinking.md) or [design-thinking.md](design-thinking.md) are the better lens
