# Design Thinking

Design thinking is a human-centered methodology for innovating on ill-defined problems: five non-linear, iterative stages — Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test — built around understanding real user needs before generating or committing to solutions. It was developed as a practice at IDEO (David Kelley, Tim Brown) and formalized into a teachable process by Stanford's d.school. A consultant needs it as the default method whenever the problem itself might be mis-framed — not "optimize this known process" but "we don't actually know what our users need," where jumping straight to solutioning just produces a well-executed answer to the wrong question.

## When to use

- The problem is ambiguous or ill-defined, and the initial framing might itself be wrong
- The client needs to innovate on customer or user experience, not just tune an internal process
- The organization keeps reapplying an old solution to a problem that has changed underneath it
- Low-cost, low-risk experimentation is possible (physical or digital prototypes) before a large investment commitment
- A cross-functional team needs a shared, structured creative process to avoid both analysis paralysis and premature convergence on the first plausible idea

## How it works

**Five stages, explicitly non-linear:**

1. **Empathize** — deep user research: interviews, immersion, observation ("shadowing"), empathy maps. Goal: set aside your own assumptions to understand the user's needs and motivations at both practical and emotional levels.
2. **Define** — synthesize the empathy research into a sharp problem statement / point-of-view: "[User] needs [need] because [insight]." Reframing the problem is the actual value the process adds — most failed innovation efforts solved the wrong problem well.
3. **Ideate** — diverge: brainstorming, brainwriting, "worst possible idea," SCAMPER. Judgment is deliberately deferred; quantity over quality at this stage, since premature filtering kills the unconventional ideas most likely to be genuinely new.
4. **Prototype** — build a scaled-down, low-fidelity version of the solution (paper sketch, wireframe, role-play, physical mockup) — cheap enough to fail and learn from without emotional or financial attachment.
5. **Test** — put the prototype in front of real users and gather feedback. Testing routinely sends the team back to Define or even Empathize with new questions — that is expected, not a failure of planning.

The d.school's own diagram draws this as a loop with arrows going backward, not a waterfall — a genuine design-thinking process visibly revisits earlier stages as new information surfaces.

**d.school mindsets that keep the process honest:** show, don't tell; focus on human values; craft clarity out of ambiguity; embrace experimentation; be mindful of process (know which stage you're in and why); bias toward action; radical collaboration across disciplines.

## Example

Doug Dietz, a GE Healthcare designer, discovered that a large share of pediatric patients were being sedated before MRI scans because they were too frightened of the machine to hold still. Empathize (observing and talking to child patients and families, not just engineers) reframed the actual problem in Define: the issue wasn't scan speed or image quality, it was fear of an intimidating machine in a cold clinical room. Ideate and Prototype produced the "Adventure Series" — repainting and re-theming the scanner room as a pirate ship or space voyage, with a script the technologist follows ("you're going into the cave, stay still so we don't wake the pirates"). Testing with real children and families drove iteration on the story and room design. The result: patient satisfaction scores rose sharply and sedation rates for pediatric scans dropped — a fix that no amount of engineering-only iteration on the scanner itself would have surfaced, because the real problem was never the machine's technical performance.

## Applying it for a client

Use it for early-stage service or product redesign engagements where the client's own framing of the problem is suspect. Insist on real field time for Empathize — a handful of actual user interviews or observations, not a boardroom session guessing at what users want — before anyone touches Define. Timebox Ideate sessions and enforce a strict no-criticism rule to protect divergence. Keep prototypes intentionally rough: client executives often want to polish a prototype into something that looks finished, which defeats the purpose (fast, cheap learning) and makes the team defensive during Test instead of receptive to bad news. Budget for at least two to three test-and-iterate cycles, not one big reveal — the process is designed to loop.

## Watch-outs

- Treating the five stages as a strict linear sequence (a stage-gate process) defeats the design — looping back is the point, not a sign of poor planning
- Skipping Empathize and starting from the team's own assumptions about users is the most common shortcut, and it reliably produces solutions nobody asked for
- Overly polished prototypes make teams emotionally attached and defensive during Test, undermining the honest feedback the stage exists to generate
- Design thinking is often reduced to "just brainstorming" — Ideate is one of five stages, and without rigorous Empathize/Define work first, ideation produces creative-but-irrelevant ideas
- It is a strong tool for human-centered, experience problems and a weaker one for problems that are primarily systemic or structural (see [systems-thinking.md](systems-thinking.md)) or purely technical and engineering-constrained
