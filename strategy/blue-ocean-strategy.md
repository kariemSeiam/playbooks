# Blue Ocean Strategy

Blue Ocean Strategy, developed by INSEAD professors W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne from a study of over 150 strategic moves across 30 industries, argues that the way to escape brutal competition isn't to out-execute rivals within the existing market — it's to make the competition irrelevant by creating a new market space nobody else is contesting. "Red oceans" are known market space where industry boundaries are defined, competitors fight over a shrinking profit pool, and products commoditize. "Blue oceans" are the untapped market space created by demand generation rather than demand capture — new categories built by breaking the assumed trade-off between differentiation and low cost. A consultant needs this because most client strategy work implicitly assumes competing *within* the industry as defined; Blue Ocean is the tool for challenging whether the industry's boundaries should be redrawn at all.

## When to use

- A client's industry has commoditized — every competitor benchmarks against every other, features converge, and the only lever left is price (a red ocean, diagnosable with [porters-five-forces.md](porters-five-forces.md) showing high rivalry and low differentiation).
- A client wants to escape a stuck-in-the-middle position (see [porters-generic-strategies.md](porters-generic-strategies.md)) not by choosing cost or differentiation, but by pursuing both simultaneously through a fundamentally different value curve.
- Launching a genuinely new offering and the client needs a structured way to ask "who are the non-customers of this industry, and what would bring them in?" rather than only benchmarking existing customers of existing competitors.
- A mature-industry client is looking for a growth story that isn't "take share from a competitor" — investors and boards often want to hear where new demand comes from, not just redistribution of existing demand.

## How it works

### Value innovation: the core idea

Blue Ocean's central mechanism is **value innovation** — simultaneously pursuing differentiation *and* low cost, rather than treating them as a trade-off (Porter's generic strategies frame them as a choice; Kim and Mauborgne argue the choice itself is the trap). Value innovation is achieved by looking at what the industry offers, then deliberately breaking the value-cost trade-off through four questions, applied to every factor the industry competes on:

### The Four Actions Framework (ERRC)

- **Eliminate** — which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated entirely? (Often factors that cost a lot to provide but customers no longer value.)
- **Reduce** — which factors should be reduced well below the industry standard? (Over-engineering to beat competitors that customers don't actually need.)
- **Raise** — which factors should be raised well above the industry standard? (Under-served aspects of the offer that would unlock real value if pushed further.)
- **Create** — which factors should be created that the industry has never offered? (New sources of value entirely outside the industry's current competitive factors.)

The Eliminate-Reduce moves are what *lower cost* (removing spend on factors that don't matter); the Raise-Create moves are what *build differentiation and new demand*. Doing all four together — not just adding features (Create alone, which raises cost) or just cutting costs (Eliminate-Reduce alone, which risks becoming a stripped-down cost play) — is what produces a genuinely new value curve instead of a repositioned old one.

### The Strategy Canvas

The strategy canvas is the diagnostic and construction tool: a chart with the industry's competing factors along the x-axis (price, features, service level, speed, convenience, etc. — whatever the industry actually competes on) and the offering level along the y-axis (low to high). Plotting every major competitor's "value curve" across those factors reveals the industry's shared strategic profile — when everyone's curve looks similar, that's the signature of a red ocean. A blue ocean move produces a curve with a fundamentally different shape: divergent (not just higher/lower on the same factors), with a clear tagline (the reader can state the new value proposition in one sentence), and it doesn't chase every existing factor — it eliminates and reduces some, while raising and creating others.

### Six Paths Framework (where to look for blue oceans)

Kim and Mauborgne's method for finding candidate reconstructions of market boundaries, deliberately looking *across* the frames most strategy work stays inside:
1. **Across alternative industries** — not just direct competitors, but products/services that solve the same underlying problem differently (a solo entrepreneur "competes" against management consultants for problem-solving budget, but is a wholly different industry).
2. **Across strategic groups within an industry** — look at what makes buyers trade up or down between price/performance tiers within the same industry.
3. **Across the chain of buyers** — the purchaser, the user, and the influencer are often different people with different needs; targeting a neglected buyer-chain link can open new value.
4. **Across complementary product/service offerings** — look at the total solution buyers seek, including what happens before, during, and after use of the product.
5. **Across functional or emotional appeal** — industries competing on function can find a blue ocean by adding emotional appeal, and vice versa.
6. **Across time** — look at trends that will reshape the industry (technology, regulation, lifestyle) with a critical, cumulative view and ask what value they'll open up.

### Three characteristics of a good strategy

Kim and Mauborgne test a resulting strategy against: **focus** (does the value curve show clear emphasis rather than trying to be all things), **divergence** (does the curve differ meaningfully from competitors' curves rather than reactively matching them), and a **compelling tagline** (can the value proposition be stated clearly and truthfully to customers) — a curve lacking any of the three signals the "strategy" is really just an incremental improvement on the red ocean.

## Example

**Cirque du Soleil — the canonical Blue Ocean case.**
The traditional circus industry (Ringling Bros. and similar) competed on: star performers, animal shows, aisle concession sales, multiple show arenas, humor/thrills, danger. It was a shrinking, low-differentiation, high-cost industry (animal care and star-performer contracts are expensive) competing for the same declining audience.

Applying ERRC:
- **Eliminate:** star performers, animal shows, aisle concessions, multiple simultaneous show arenas — all costly, none valued equally by the new target audience.
- **Reduce:** humor and danger — retained in lighter form, no longer the core draw.
- **Raise:** a unique venue/tent experience.
- **Create:** theme, refined artistic environment, and — crucially — the combination of theatrical storytelling and Broadway-style production values with circus artistry, targeting adults and corporate clients rather than children.

The resulting value curve diverged sharply from every traditional circus competitor: lower cost (no animal care, no star salaries) *and* higher perceived value (theatrical sophistication, new pricing tier), simultaneously — the exact break from the value-cost trade-off that defines value innovation. Cirque du Soleil didn't take share from Ringling Bros.; it created a new demand pool (adults willing to pay premium ticket prices for a theater-like experience) that hadn't been circus customers before.

## Applying it for a client

Start with the strategy canvas as a diagnostic before proposing anything — plot the client's current offer against 2-3 real competitors across the factors the industry actually competes on (get this list from the client's sales team, not from a generic template) and show it back to them. If every curve looks the same, that's the evidence a red ocean exists, and it's usually more persuasive to the client than saying "you're commoditized." Then run ERRC as a live workshop, in that literal order — Eliminate and Reduce first, because clients instinctively want to jump straight to Create (add a new feature) without funding it by cutting something else, which just adds cost onto an already-crowded offer rather than producing a genuinely new curve. Use the Six Paths systematically rather than brainstorming freely — ask the buyer-chain question and the complementary-offerings question explicitly, since those two paths surface non-obvious opportunities a client's internal team, immersed in their own industry's assumptions, rarely raises unprompted. Close by testing the resulting concept against focus, divergence, and a one-sentence tagline — if the client can't state the new value proposition simply, the ERRC work likely produced an improved red-ocean offer, not a blue ocean.

## Watch-outs

- Blue Ocean is not "innovation" in general — an offering that's merely differentiated (Raise/Create without genuine Eliminate/Reduce) still competes on the old cost structure and isn't a blue ocean; the framework's whole premise is breaking the value-cost trade-off, not just adding value at added cost.
- Blue oceans don't stay blue — first-mover advantage erodes as competitors imitate the reconstructed value curve (this happened to Cirque du Soleil itself, and to Southwest Airlines relative to legacy carriers); treat it as a strategic reset, not a permanent moat, and pair with [vrio-framework.md](vrio-framework.md) to assess how imitable the new position actually is.
- The framework is retrospectively easy to narrate (every case study looks obvious after the fact) and prospectively hard to execute — most attempted "blue ocean" pitches from clients turn out, under a real strategy-canvas test, to be repositioned red-ocean offers; hold the discipline of actually plotting competitor curves rather than accepting the client's self-assessment.
- Works best for challenger or resource-constrained clients precisely because it doesn't require outspending an incumbent — but it does require genuine willingness to eliminate revenue-generating factors the org is emotionally attached to, which is the step clients resist most.
- Not a substitute for industry-structure analysis — a client still needs to understand the Five Forces of wherever the new value curve lands them, because a blue ocean that succeeds attracts new entrants and eventually becomes a red one.
