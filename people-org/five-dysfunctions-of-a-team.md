# The Five Dysfunctions of a Team

Patrick Lencioni's model, published as a leadership fable in 2002, argues that team dysfunction is never really about skill, resources, or strategy — it's a pyramid of five interlocking failures, each one built on the one beneath it, and you cannot fix a layer higher up without first repairing the layer it sits on. It matters to a consultant because it reframes almost every "our team just doesn't execute" complaint as a diagnosable, ordered problem: teams don't fail at results because they're bad at results, they fail because a missing foundation (usually trust) makes every layer above it unstable. Two decades and millions of copies later, it remains one of the most cited team-effectiveness frameworks in leadership consulting — genuinely battle-tested, if occasionally over-applied as a checklist rather than a diagnostic.

## When to use

- A leadership team or department is technically competent but consistently underperforms, misses deadlines, or produces mediocre outcomes despite good individual talent.
- Meetings are polite and conflict-free on the surface, but real disagreement happens in hallway conversations afterward ("artificial harmony").
- Decisions get made in meetings and then quietly re-litigated or ignored once people leave the room.
- Peers won't call each other out on missed commitments or subpar work, leaving that job entirely to the manager.
- Individual or departmental ego and status visibly outweigh the team's shared scoreboard — people optimize for how they look, not for whether the team wins.

## How it works

### The pyramid, bottom to top

Each dysfunction listed below is a *consequence* of the one beneath it — read the pyramid upward, and diagnose it upward too: don't try to fix "results" before checking whether "trust" is actually present.

| Layer (bottom→top) | Dysfunction | What it looks like | What it produces above it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Absence of Trust** | Team members are unwilling to be vulnerable with each other — admitting mistakes, weaknesses, or asking for help feels unsafe. Lencioni specifically means *vulnerability-based* trust, not "predictive" trust (I trust you'll act a certain way) — I trust you enough to be exposed in front of you. | Without it, people posture instead of engaging honestly, which blocks real conflict. |
| 2 | **Fear of Conflict** | Because there's no trust, the team avoids passionate, unfiltered debate about ideas and defaults to "artificial harmony" — polite meetings, guarded comments, real disagreement happening only in side conversations afterward. | Without real debate, people never fully engage with a decision, so they don't truly buy in. |
| 3 | **Lack of Commitment** | Without honest conflict, decisions are made without genuine buy-in — people nod in the room but don't feel ownership, so ambiguity and lingering disagreement persist. | Without clear commitment to a decision, there's nothing concrete to hold each other accountable *to*. |
| 4 | **Avoidance of Accountability** | Because commitment was never real, team members avoid calling out peers on behaviors or performance that hurt the team — accountability becomes something only the manager does, one-directional instead of peer-to-peer. | Without peer accountability, individuals protect their own status rather than the team's collective outcome. |
| 5 | **Inattention to Results** | Team members prioritize individual ego, career, or department status over the team's collective goals — "my numbers looked fine" substitutes for "did we actually win." | This is the top-line symptom every struggling team complains about — but it's the last domino, not the first. |

### Why the order matters diagnostically

The most common mistake a consultant sees is a client trying to fix layer 5 (results) or layer 4 (accountability) directly — installing a scorecard, or telling people to "hold each other accountable" — without ever repairing layer 1 (trust). Peer accountability is *impossible* to sustain if the team never got real commitment from genuine conflict, which never happens if trust isn't present. Interventions have to start at the lowest broken layer.

### Practical interventions per layer

- **Building trust** — structured vulnerability exercises (e.g., a "personal histories" exercise where each member shares formative background, strengths, and a genuine weakness), team effectiveness reviews where members give each other direct feedback.
- **Mining for conflict** — an explicit facilitator role that draws out buried disagreement in real time ("I sense there's a disagreement here — let's have it"), separating idea-conflict from personal-conflict so debate stays productive.
- **Forcing commitment** — closing every significant meeting with clarity on decisions made and by when, and a cascading message so every team communicates the same decision downward within 24 hours, before ambiguity re-forms.
- **Enabling accountability** — publishing clear standards and progress publicly, scheduled peer-review moments (not just top-down performance reviews), and a team norm that calling out a missed commitment is a sign of respect, not aggression.
- **Focusing on results** — a visible, shared scoreboard tied to collective goals, with individual recognition made explicitly secondary to team outcomes in how success is celebrated.

## Example

A 7-person exec team at a mid-size logistics company reports "we keep missing quarterly targets despite everyone working hard." Surface diagnosis suggests layer 5 (Inattention to Results) — but tracing downward: peers never challenge each other's numbers in the leadership meeting (layer 4, Avoidance of Accountability), because the last time someone pushed back on the Sales VP's forecast, it turned into a tense standoff that never got resolved (layer 3, Lack of Commitment — nobody actually bought into the reconciled number, they just stopped arguing). That standoff happened because the meeting culture treats disagreement as a threat rather than useful friction (layer 2, Fear of Conflict) — which traces back to a CEO who was previously blindsided by a VP's mistake and now visibly distrusts direct reports who admit uncertainty (layer 1, Absence of Trust). The intervention starts at layer 1: a facilitated trust-building session where the CEO models admitting a past misjudgment, not a new KPI dashboard at layer 5.

## Applying it for a client

Run a short, anonymous team assessment (Lencioni publishes a standard one) before recommending any intervention — it's fast, and it tells you which layer is actually broken instead of guessing from the loudest surface symptom. Resist the client's instinct to jump straight to layer 4 or 5 ("we just need better accountability," "let's set clearer KPIs") if the assessment shows trust or conflict-avoidance as the real floor — a scorecard imposed on a team that doesn't trust each other just becomes one more thing people quietly game. Sequence the intervention: don't run a "commitment workshop" before a trust exercise has actually happened, and don't expect a single offsite to fix all five layers — trust-building in particular is a sustained practice (regular team effectiveness reviews), not a one-time event. When working with an executive team specifically, watch the leader's own behavior first — Lencioni is explicit that the leader must model vulnerability-based trust before asking the team to.

## Watch-outs

- Treating the pyramid as a one-time offsite exercise rather than an ongoing practice — trust and healthy conflict decay without reinforcement, especially after a new hire joins or a stressful period (layoffs, a missed target) resets group dynamics.
- Confusing "no visible arguments" with "healthy team" — artificial harmony (layer 2) often looks calmer and more professional than a team in productive conflict, which is exactly why it's under-diagnosed.
- Applying accountability interventions (layer 4) without commitment (layer 3) in place — peer accountability without genuine buy-in to the thing being enforced reads as policing, not partnership, and breeds resentment.
- A single toxic or highly political member can hold an entire team at layer 1 regardless of what the other six people do — the model assumes good-faith participants; it isn't a fix for one person actively working against the team's interest.
- Overusing the framework as a label ("we're a layer-2 team") rather than a diagnostic starting point — the value is in the sequenced intervention, not in the taxonomy itself.
