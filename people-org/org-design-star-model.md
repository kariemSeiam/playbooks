# Organization Design — The Star Model and Mintzberg's Configurations

Jay Galbraith's Star Model holds that an organization's design isn't just its org chart — it's five interdependent policy areas that all have to point the same direction, and changing one without touching the other four is the single most common reorg failure. It matters to a consultant because clients almost always ask for a *structure* fix (a new reporting line, a reorg chart) when the actual dysfunction lives in a mismatch between structure and one of the other four points — usually Rewards or Processes, which nobody thought to change when the boxes on the chart moved. Complementing it, Henry Mintzberg's five organizational configurations describe recognizable "shapes" a company's design tends to settle into depending on its size, environment, and coordination needs — useful for naming what a client's org *already is* before prescribing what it should become. Both are battle-tested, decades-old staples of organization-design consulting.

## When to use

- A client just reorganized (or is about to) and wants to know if the new structure will actually work, not just look cleaner on a chart.
- Teams are structurally aligned to a new strategy but still behave the old way — usually a sign Rewards or Processes weren't updated to match.
- Diagnosing why a company "feels" disorganized: is it actually a Structure problem, or is Structure fine and the real gap is in cross-functional Processes or misaligned Rewards?
- A founder wants to know what kind of organization they're building (or should be building) as they scale past the founding team.
- Comparing a client's current org shape against the shape their strategy and environment actually demand (Mintzberg's configurations).

## How it works

### The Star Model — five points that must align

Galbraith's model is a star, not a hierarchy — no point is "first," and the model's central claim is that misalignment between any two points creates dysfunction, regardless of which point is individually well-designed.

| Point | Question it answers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** | What capabilities and priorities matter most for winning? | Direction of growth (new markets vs. deepening existing ones), what to differentiate on |
| **Structure** | Where does formal power and specialization sit? | Functional, divisional, matrix, or front-back hybrid structures; how many layers; span of control |
| **Processes** | How does information and work actually flow? | *Vertical* processes (budgeting, business planning — allocating resources top-down) and *horizontal* processes (lateral coordination across silos — the mechanisms that let a cross-functional launch actually happen without a reorg) |
| **Rewards** | What does the org actually pay, promote, and recognize people for? | Compensation structure, promotion criteria, bonus metrics, informal status signals |
| **People (practices)** | What skills, mindsets, and behaviors does the org recruit, develop, and rotate for? | Hiring profiles, training investment, rotation programs, career paths |

The model's operating rule: **strategy determines structure, and structure alone is insufficient** — Processes, Rewards, and People all have to be deliberately redesigned around a new Structure, or the new boxes on the chart will produce old behavior. A common example: a company reorganizes from functional silos into cross-functional squads (Structure changes) but keeps promoting and bonusing people on individual functional output (Rewards unchanged) — the squads exist on paper, but everyone still optimizes for their home function, because that's what actually gets rewarded.

### Mintzberg's organizational configurations

Mintzberg described recognizable "species" of organization, each with a dominant coordinating mechanism and a part of the org that holds the real power:

| Configuration | Dominant coordination | Typical setting | Power sits in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple Structure** | Direct supervision | Small, young, or crisis-driven organizations; founder-led startups | The top (owner/founder) |
| **Machine Bureaucracy** | Standardized work processes | Manufacturing, mass production, regulated back-office operations | The technostructure (planners, standard-setters) |
| **Professional Bureaucracy** | Standardized skills | Hospitals, universities, law and consulting firms | The operating core (the credentialed professionals) |
| **Divisionalized Form** | Standardized outputs | Large multi-business conglomerates, holding companies | Middle management (division heads) |
| **Adhocracy** | Mutual adjustment | Innovation-driven agencies, R&D units, early-stage tech, creative studios | Shifting project teams, no fixed locus |

No configuration is inherently better — each is a fit (or mismatch) for a specific strategy and environment. A Machine Bureaucracy running an innovation strategy will strangle it in standardized process; an Adhocracy trying to scale a highly regulated, safety-critical operation will produce chaos where consistency was actually required.

## Example

A 200-person branding agency reorganizes from functional departments (Strategy, Creative, Production, Accounts) into client-facing "pods" — each pod a small cross-functional team owning a set of client relationships end to end (Structure changes toward something closer to Adhocracy). Six months later, the reorg is judged a failure: pods complain they can't get creative resourcing decisions made without escalating to the old Creative department head, and top creative talent still angles for promotion into that same head role rather than pod leadership. Running it through the Star Model: Structure changed, but Rewards didn't (promotion path still runs through the old functional hierarchy) and Processes didn't (resourcing decisions still flow through the old vertical Creative approval chain, not a new horizontal process the pods can use directly). The chart says "pods," but Rewards and Processes still say "functional department" — that mismatch, not the pod concept itself, is what's failing.

## Applying it for a client

Before endorsing or designing a structural change, walk all five points explicitly with the client and ask, for each one, "does this still make sense after the Structure changes?" — most reorg failures a consultant is called in to fix are Structure-only changes where Rewards or Processes were left untouched, and the fix is rarely "reorganize again," it's "finish the redesign you already started." Use Mintzberg's configurations as a naming exercise early in an engagement — asking a client "which of these five does your org actually behave like today, regardless of what the chart says" often surfaces a gap between the org's real coordinating mechanism (frequently Simple Structure, direct supervision by the founder, well past the size where that scales) and the configuration its stated strategy requires. For a scaling startup specifically, the most common Mintzberg transition to watch for is Simple Structure outgrowing itself — founders who still personally approve most decisions once the org passes ~30-50 people, producing a bottleneck the org chart doesn't reveal.

## Watch-outs

- Redesigning Structure without touching Rewards is the single most common Star Model failure in practice — always check what the org actually pays and promotes people for before declaring a reorg complete.
- The Star Model has no "correct" configuration — it's a fit test against Strategy, not a universal template; a matrix structure that's right for one client's strategy is actively wrong for another's.
- Horizontal Processes (lateral coordination) are the point most often skipped entirely in a redesign, because they're less visible than an org chart — a new cross-functional Structure without new lateral processes to support it just recreates old silo behavior with new team names.
- Mintzberg's configurations are archetypes, not boxes — most real organizations are hybrids or sit mid-transition between two configurations (e.g., a Professional Bureaucracy growing a Machine Bureaucracy back-office); forcing a client into exactly one label oversimplifies.
- People practices (hiring, development, rotation) lag behind Structure changes by default, since recruiting pipelines and training programs were built for the old shape — budget for this lag explicitly rather than assuming People will self-correct once Structure changes.
