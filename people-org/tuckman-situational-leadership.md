# Tuckman's Stages of Team Development and Situational Leadership

Two older, battle-tested models pair naturally together: Bruce Tuckman's stages describe the predictable *arc a team moves through over time* (Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing, and — added later with Mary Ann Jensen in 1977 — Adjourning), while Paul Hersey and Kenneth Blanchard's Situational Leadership model describes how a *leader's style should adapt to an individual follower's readiness* on a given task (Directing, Coaching, Supporting, Delegating, matched to Readiness levels R1-R4). A consultant needs both because they answer different but related questions a client always eventually asks: "why does my team feel different than it did three months ago" (Tuckman) and "why does the same management style work for one person on my team and completely fail with another" (Hersey-Blanchard). Used together, they let you diagnose a team's current stage and prescribe a leadership style that fits both the team's stage and each individual's readiness on their specific task — a manager over-directing a Performing team, or under-supporting someone in Storming, is a mismatch this pairing makes visible.

## When to use

- A new team (a fresh hire, a new project group, a post-reorg team) needs a manager to set expectations about the friction that's coming, not just the smooth state they hope to reach.
- A previously high-functioning team suddenly feels tense or unproductive — often a regression to Storming triggered by a new member, a leadership change, or a stressful external event, not a sign the team has fundamentally broken.
- A manager applies one leadership style uniformly across their whole team and gets wildly inconsistent results — a sign they need Hersey-Blanchard's per-person, per-task calibration instead of a single default style.
- Onboarding a capable new hire who's nonetheless being either micromanaged (wrong style for their readiness) or thrown in the deep end with no support (also wrong style for their readiness).
- A project team is winding down (a fixed-term engagement, a completed initiative) and needs deliberate closure — the Adjourning stage most managers never plan for.

## How it works

### Tuckman's stages of team development

| Stage | What's happening | Team behavior | Leader's job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Forming** | Team is new; members are polite, cautious, and unsure of roles or each other | High dependence on the leader for direction; conflict avoided; members test boundaries quietly | Provide clear structure, purpose, and roles — direction, not democracy, is what's needed here |
| **Storming** | Real work begins and real differences surface — over priorities, roles, working styles, or the leader's authority itself | Conflict, pushback, cliques, frustration; some teams stall here indefinitely if unmanaged | Coach through conflict actively — don't suppress it (see [five-dysfunctions-of-a-team.md](five-dysfunctions-of-a-team.md) on the cost of artificial harmony); keep providing direction while inviting real input |
| **Norming** | The team works through Storming's friction and settles on shared norms, roles, and working agreements | Increasing trust and cohesion; conflict starts resolving faster; people begin covering for each other | Shift from directing to supporting — the team needs less top-down structure and more encouragement to keep the emerging norms alive |
| **Performing** | The team operates with high trust, flexible roles, and minimal need for supervision | High autonomy, high output, team self-corrects most problems without escalation | Delegate broadly — heavy oversight at this stage is a drag on a team that has earned autonomy |
| **Adjourning** (added 1977) | The team disbands — project ends, org changes, members move on | Can include a real sense of loss or anxiety about what's next, especially for long-running or high-intensity teams | Create deliberate closure — retrospectives, explicit recognition, transition support — rather than letting the team simply dissolve |

Teams don't always move through the stages linearly — a new member, a leadership change, or an external shock (a missed deadline, a re-org) can push a Performing team back into Storming, and that regression is normal, not evidence the team was never really Performing.

### Hersey-Blanchard Situational Leadership

The model's central claim: there is no single best leadership style — the right style depends on the follower's **readiness** (competence + commitment) for the *specific task* at hand, not a fixed trait of the person overall. Someone can be R4 (fully ready) on a task they've done for years and R1 (not ready) on a brand-new one.

| Readiness level | Competence | Commitment | Matching style | Leader behavior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **R1** | Low | High (enthusiastic beginner) | **S1 — Directing/Telling** | High direction, low support: tell them specifically what to do and how |
| **R2** | Some | Low (disillusioned learner — early enthusiasm has met real difficulty) | **S2 — Coaching/Selling** | High direction, high support: keep directing, but now also explain the why and rebuild motivation |
| **R3** | High | Variable (capable but cautious or insecure) | **S3 — Supporting/Participating** | Low direction, high support: they know how; they need encouragement and shared decision-making, not instructions |
| **R4** | High | High (self-reliant achiever) | **S4 — Delegating** | Low direction, low support: hand over the task and get out of the way |

The practical failure mode this model targets directly: a manager who defaults to one style regardless of readiness — either micromanaging an R4 performer (breeds resentment and disengagement) or delegating to an R1 beginner with no direction (breeds anxiety and poor-quality output, since low competence combined with low support produces mistakes, not growth).

### How the two models map onto each other

Applied to a *team's* overall stage rather than one person's task readiness, there's a natural (though not literally identical) correspondence a consultant can use as a working heuristic: Forming pairs with Directing (the team needs clear structure, like an R1 follower); Storming pairs with Coaching (real direction plus real support to work through friction, like an R2 follower rebuilding commitment); Norming pairs with Supporting (the team knows what to do, needs encouragement and shared ownership, like R3); Performing pairs with Delegating (high trust, minimal oversight, like R4). This mapping is a practitioner synthesis for talking about team-level leadership adaptation — Tuckman and Hersey-Blanchard were developed independently and don't formally reference each other — but it's a useful bridge between "what stage is this team in" and "what style should I default to while they're in it."

## Example

A newly-formed 5-person analytics team is assembled for a client engagement. Week 1 (**Forming**): the lead sets explicit roles, a clear project charter, and daily check-ins — high direction (S1), because the team doesn't yet have shared norms to lean on. Week 3 (**Storming**): two analysts clash over whose modeling approach the team should standardize on, and one member starts quietly missing check-ins. The lead doesn't suppress the disagreement — they facilitate an explicit debate to resolve the modeling-approach conflict and have a direct 1:1 with the disengaging member, discovering they feel their expertise isn't being used (a **Coaching**, S2, response — direction plus rebuilding commitment). By Week 6 (**Norming**), the team has agreed on a shared methodology and starts covering gaps for each other without being asked; the lead shifts to weekly rather than daily check-ins and starts asking the team for its own read on quality (**Supporting**, S3). By Week 10 (**Performing**), the lead is delegating full client-facing sub-workstreams to team members with minimal review (**Delegating**, S4). At the engagement's close (**Adjourning**), the lead runs a retrospective and explicitly names each member's growth over the project rather than letting the team disband with a final Slack message.

## Applying it for a client

When a client reports "my new team is a mess," check whether it's actually a *normal* Storming stage rather than evidence the team was badly assembled — the diagnostic question is whether conflict is emerging over legitimate substance (priorities, roles, approach) as opposed to personal dysfunction, and whether it's being surfaced or suppressed; teams that skip Storming by suppressing conflict often stall permanently rather than reach Norming (again, see [five-dysfunctions-of-a-team.md](five-dysfunctions-of-a-team.md)). When coaching a manager on leadership style, use Hersey-Blanchard to break the "I'm a hands-off manager" or "I'm a hands-on manager" self-description — most managers have a stated style preference that doesn't actually track each report's real readiness per task, and the fastest improvement is often just matching style to the specific person and specific task rather than changing their overall philosophy. For project-based consulting engagements specifically, plan the Adjourning stage deliberately from the start — a fixed-term client team that disbands with no closure ritual loses institutional knowledge and, for members who invested heavily, can produce real disengagement on their next assignment.

## Watch-outs

- Assuming Storming is a one-time stage a team passes through once and never returns to — a new hire, a leadership change, or an external shock can regress a Performing team back to Storming, and that's expected, not a failure of the earlier stages.
- Applying a single leadership style to a whole team rather than per-person, per-task — Hersey-Blanchard's readiness levels are specific to a task, not a fixed trait; a strong performer on one workstream can genuinely be an R1 beginner on a new one.
- Confusing "high commitment" with "high readiness" — an R1 enthusiastic beginner has high commitment but low competence, and over-delegating to them (treating enthusiasm as readiness) sets them up to fail, which then *creates* the low-commitment R2 dip the model predicts.
- Skipping Adjourning entirely, especially in project-based or consulting contexts where teams are inherently temporary — the lack of deliberate closure is a common, avoidable source of morale loss on the next engagement.
- Treating the Tuckman/Hersey-Blanchard mapping in "how it works" as literal or original to either author — it's a practical bridge for talking about team-level style, not a claim that Tuckman and Hersey-Blanchard designed their models together.
