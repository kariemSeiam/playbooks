# Customer Value, Cost, Convenience, Communication (4Cs)

The 4Cs are the customer-centric rewrite of the marketing mix's 4Ps (Product→Customer Value, Price→Cost, Place→Convenience, Promotion→Communication) — a lens for analyzing competitors and your own offering from the buyer's seat instead of the seller's. It matters because most competitor analysis defaults to product-centric, seller-perspective comparison ("what features do they have that we don't"); the 4Cs force a customer-centric, buyer-perspective read ("what does the customer actually get, at what cost to them, how easily, and how do they even find out about it").

## When to use

Use the 4Cs specifically as the framework for competitor analysis and value-proposition definition — it's step 4 ("analyse competitors") inside the broader 7-step market analysis process below. Reach for it whenever you need to answer "why would a customer choose us over the alternative," not just "what does our product do."

## How it works

**Market analysis is a 7-step process**, and the 4Cs live inside step 4:

1. Define the objective
2. Conduct industry research
3. Identify target segment
4. **Analyse competitors** (via the 4Cs — see below)
5. Customer research (interviews/surveys — see [mvp-customer-validation.md](mvp-customer-validation.md))
6. SWOT analysis (Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats — internal + external factors)
7. Findings & conclusion

**Why analyze competitors specifically as product (not just as marketing)?** Outcomes: understand the market and offerings, identify differing value propositions, identify gaps, understand market trends, optimize pricing strategy. The shift the 4Cs force is from **product-centric → customer-centric** and **seller perspective → buyer perspective**.

**The 4Cs, each broken into "what we want to know," "aspects to consider," and the outcome/action plan it produces:**

1. **Customer Value** (maps to Product)
   - Know: customer needs/wants, value offered by competitors, problems users face
   - Consider: value proposition, service description, key features, key differentiators (innovation/uniqueness), region coverage, B2B sales volume
   - Produces: our segment, our value proposition, must-have features, competitive edge (how we differentiate)

2. **Cost** (maps to Price)
   - Know: how much money a customer is willing to exchange for satisfaction
   - Consider: pricing model (in-app purchase, subscription, transaction fees), learning curve (time/effort to use the product), discounts/promotions, B2B commission structures
   - Produces: our pricing strategy and revenue model

3. **Convenience** (maps to Place)
   - Know: ease, accessibility, and user-friendliness of using the product to fulfill the need
   - Consider: onboarding, in-app guides, ease of use/interface, payment methods, refund policy, cancel/amend policy, responsiveness/reliability/accessibility, personalization, customer support, in-app messaging, post-purchase experience, B2B contracting requirements, ease of communication with admins
   - Produces: list of features needed for a user-friendly app; a seamless end-to-end experience

4. **Communication** (maps to Promotion)
   - Know: how competitors communicate their value to customers; which channels they use to engage users
   - Consider: positioning, notifications/alerts, push notifications, frequency of communication, channels of communication
   - Produces: channels this segment prefers; how we'll communicate our value; how product value gets delivered through in-app features

## Example

Two ride-hailing competitors both offer "a car that takes you from A to B" (same Product/4P read — indistinguishable). Run the same comparison through the 4Cs: Customer Value — one offers door-to-door reliability, the other offers the cheapest possible fare; Cost — one has surge pricing, the other flat-rate; Convenience — one has a five-tap booking flow, the other requires manual address entry every time; Communication — one sends proactive "your driver is 2 minutes away" pushes, the other is silent until arrival. The 4C read surfaces exactly where the real competitive gap is (convenience and communication, not the core product), which the 4P read would have missed entirely.

## Applying it for a client

Run the 4Cs as a structured competitor teardown in a client workshop: pull 2-3 direct competitors, and for each, fill in one row per C using only public information (app store listings, pricing pages, app walkthroughs, review-site complaints). For a clinic client, "Convenience" is very often the entire battlefield — an incumbent clinic's differentiator is rarely a better doctor (Customer Value parity is common in healthcare), it's booking friction, wait times, and post-visit communication. For a branding agency doing a repositioning project, the 4C table becomes the evidence base for the messaging brief — "Communication" gaps you find in competitors are the openings your client's campaign should claim first, before a competitor closes them.

## Watch-outs

- Don't stop at Customer Value (the old "Product" comparison) — most teams over-invest analysis time there and skim Convenience and Communication, which is usually where the actual differentiation opportunity is hiding.
- The 4Cs describe competitors' *positioning*, not necessarily the truth of their product quality — validate perception (what customers believe) separately from the competitor's actual delivery.
- SWOT (step 6) and the 4Cs (step 4) address different questions — SWOT is about your own strategic position (including internal factors like resources and capability), the 4Cs are about market/competitive perception from the customer's seat. Don't merge them into one exercise; run both.
