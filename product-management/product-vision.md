# Product Vision

A product vision is the big-picture destination the organization is steering toward — a long-term (3-10 year) statement of what you want to achieve, not a plan for how you'll get there. It matters because it's the one artifact every other strategic decision (BMC, roadmap, prioritization) gets checked against: if a feature doesn't serve the vision, that's a reason to say no to it.

## When to use

Write or revisit a vision statement at company/product founding, after a pivot, when a new product line launches, or whenever a team's day-to-day decisions have started drifting in different directions because there's no shared destination to check against. It's the first of the eight product-strategy elements (vision, market analysis, BMC, persona, customer lifecycle, metrics/KPIs, roadmap, prioritization) — everything downstream should be traceable back to it.

## How it works

**What a vision is:**

1. The big picture of what you want to achieve
2. A destination for the organization — not a to-do list, not a roadmap

**Why it matters:**

1. Inspires and motivates the team and stakeholders
2. Anchors long-term goal-setting so short-term decisions don't drift

**How to write an effective vision statement:**

1. Be clear — avoid complex wording
2. Make it simple enough that everyone in the org understands it
3. Use language that inspires and motivates people
4. Include a sense of purpose, optimism, and excitement
5. Focus on long-term impact — 3 to 10 years out, not this quarter

A vision statement is deliberately short (one sentence, typically) and deliberately non-operational — it says nothing about *how*, *when*, or *for how much*. That's what separates it from a roadmap or a strategy document: the vision doesn't change often; the plans to reach it change constantly.

## Example

Two vision statements from the deck, both short and aspirational rather than descriptive of current features:

- **Uber:** "We reimagine the way the world moves for the better."
- **Disney+:** "To be one of the world's leading producers and providers of entertainment."

Neither mentions ride-hailing apps, drivers, or streaming catalogs specifically — that's the point. The vision describes the destination; the business model, roadmap, and features are how you get there, and those can change without the vision needing to.

## Applying it for a client

For a clinic client, a vision like "make specialist-quality care reachable for every family in the region" gives you a filter for later roadmap fights (does telehealth serve this? yes — prioritize; does a loyalty-points feature serve this? no — deprioritize regardless of how fun it is to build). For a branding agency positioning itself as a product/strategy partner (not just a design shop), a vision statement is often the first deliverable that reveals whether the client actually has strategic clarity — if leadership can't agree on one sentence in a workshop, that's diagnostic information worth surfacing before any deeper work starts. Draft 3 candidate vision statements in a session, test each against "does this exclude anything, and does it inspire," and let the client pick — don't hand them a finished statement cold.

## Watch-outs

- A vision that describes current product features ("we make a ride-hailing app") isn't a vision — it's a product description, and it will need rewriting the moment the product changes.
- Vague inspirational language without a discernible boundary ("we make the world better") is equally useless — it can't say no to anything, so it can't guide prioritization.
- Don't confuse vision with mission or values — the deck treats vision specifically as *destination*, distinct from the roadmap (the path) and the BMC (the mechanics of how you'll sustain the journey).
